What if the most important model training the next generation of AI is not a human but the previous generation of AI?
For most of the modern AI boom, the race looked like a contest of scale: more data, more chips and larger training runs. But the next durable advantage may be less visible the private system that turns today’s model into a researcher, evaluator and teacher for tomorrow’s.
This is the practical promise of recursive self-improvement, or RSI: a loop connecting models, harnesses, synthetic data, evaluators and compute, with each generation helping to build the next. Full RSI has not yet been demonstrated, but its essential components are rapidly moving from theory into frontier AI labs.
From an intelligence explosion to an engineering roadmap
In 1965, statistician I. J. Good described an “intelligence explosion”: a sufficiently capable machine could help design a superior machine, which could then repeat the process.Jürgen Schmidhuber later formalized a related idea in the Gödel Machine, a theoretical agent that could rewrite its own software after proving that a modification would improve its expected performance.
Modern systems are more constrained. Rather than rewriting their neural-network weights directly, they improve answers, generate data, write code or optimize the software surrounding a fixed model. The path toward RSI is therefore a gradual closing of the feedback loop.
Three concepts are often conflated. Self-refinement revises an answer during inference. Iterative self-training learns from model-generated solutions or judgments. Full RSI requires improvements that increase the capacity to produce further, inheritable improvements.
RLHF and RLVR: from human taste to machine-checkable progress
Reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF, made human preferences into a practical training signal. People demonstrate desired behavior or rank outputs; a reward model learns those preferences; and the language model is optimized against that reward. The approach emerged in 2017 and became central to language-model post-training with systems such as InstructGPT.
RLHF works when quality is subjective helpfulness, relevance or safety but human judgment is expensive, inconsistent and potentially inadequate for supervising superhuman work.
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR, adds another channel. In mathematics, programming and formal reasoning, answers can be checked by proof verifiers, compilers, unit tests or known solutions. 
Process-supervision research examined intermediate steps, while DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated large-scale reinforcement learning with rule-based rewards.
RLVR does not replace RLHF. It works best where correctness is mechanically testable; RLHF remains important where goals depend on human values. Together, however, they reveal the central requirement of every self-improvement loop:  the ability to tell a genuine improvement from a persuasive failure .
The harness: an operating system for intelligence
Between a raw model and an autonomous agent sits the harness. @lilianweng defines a harness as the surrounding system that controls how a model plans, calls tools, manages context, stores artifacts, delegates work and evaluates results. It is closer to a runtime or operating system than to a clever prompt.
Near-term self-improvement may therefore begin outside the weights. A model can improve its instructions, memory, tools or workflow. A better harness enables more reliable work and may improve the training pipeline for a successor model.
Synthetic data: how a harness creates its own curriculum
A harness also needs a steady supply of tasks on which to train and test itself. Synthetic data can provide that supply but only when generation and verification are designed together. In the AutoData workflow discussed by Weng, a main agent uses grounding data to direct a challenger model, which proposes new examples. A strong solver, weak solver and verifier then evaluate each candidate.
The target is a “just-right” task: difficult enough that the weak solver fails, but structured enough that the strong solver succeeds and the verifier can check the result. Accepted examples become synthetic training or evaluation data. This creates an adaptive curriculum around the system’s current capability boundary instead of repeatedly testing it on a static benchmark.
AutoData workflow for generating synthetic training and evaluation data around challenger, solver and verifier roles. Source: Kulikov et al. (2026), as presented by Weng (2026).
This is especially useful for harness testing. Failure traces can be converted into new challenges, proposed harness changes can be evaluated against them, and held-out synthetic tasks can test whether an apparent fix generalizes. AutoData also meta-optimizes the data-producing agent, so additional inference-time search can produce better future datasets. But the loop is still bounded: Weng notes that the demonstrated system trains weaker solvers rather than recursively upgrading the strongest model. Synthetic tests must also remain anchored to real or independently held-out data, or the harness may simply become better at passing challenges it created itself.
A possible frontier-lab example follows the same teacher–student pattern. MindStudio reports that GPT‑5.6 Sol was used to post-train GPT‑5.6 Luna, generating synthetic examples, evaluating Luna’s outputs and supplying feedback in place of some human ratings. If accurate, this is better described as AI-assisted post-training or distillation than full RSI: a stronger model helps improve a smaller related model inside a pipeline whose objectives and boundaries remain human-designed.
The interface can make this transition look simple. Codex’s experimental `/goal` feature turns a request into a durable objective. Anthropic’s Claude Code `/loop` and related “Ralph loop” patterns return an agent to a task until a completion condition is satisfied. These commands are not RSI, but they expose its control primitives: a goal, repetition, memory, verification and a stopping rule.
OpenAI describes the Codex harness as the layer orchestrating the user, model and tools while managing persistent threads, sandboxed execution, skills and integrations. In one internal experiment, OpenAI reported that a small team used Codex to create roughly one million lines of software without manually writing code. The key investment was an agent-legible repository with tests, architectural rules, observability and automated review.
 When the agent becomes the researcher
AutoResearch systems extend the same loop from software development into AI development. @karpathy AutoResearch gives an agent a small language-model training setup, a fixed experiment budget and one validation metric. The agent edits the training code, runs an experiment and keeps or discards the change.
Other projects close more of the loop. The AI Scientist generates research ideas, writes code, executes experiments and produces papers.  AlphaEvolve  uses language models and evolutionary search to discover verifiable programs. The  Darwin Gödel Machine  modifies its own coding-agent harness and retains descendants that perform better on coding benchmarks.
These remain bounded systems: humans still supply their base models, objectives, compute and evaluators. They nevertheless show models searching architectures, training recipes, kernels and agent designs at high speed.
RSI becomes a company and a frontier-lab strategy
@Recursive_SI  makes this its explicit mission: first build AI that improves AI, then apply it to other sciences. In June 2026, it reported improvements in fixed-budget model training, a small-model speedrun and GPU-kernel optimization. These are early, company-reported results, but Recursive also documents the central failure mode: candidates sometimes exploited the evaluator rather than solving the intended problem.
Frontier labs are moving in the same direction. OpenAI lists an automated AI researcher  among its principal goals and believes AI could perform a significant fraction of its research alongside humans by March 2028. PaperBench and MLE-bench measure paper replication and ML engineering, while OpenAI’s system cards group internal research debugging and kernel optimization under “AI Self-Improvement.”
Anthropic is more direct about the destination. Its 2026 essay  When AI Builds Itself  says AI is already accelerating parts of AI development, while emphasizing that full RSI has not arrived and may not be inevitable. Anthropic has also built an Automated Weak-to-Strong Researcher whose agents propose ideas and run experiments on a narrow, outcome-gradable alignment problem.
This is why the loop may become the moat. A frontier lab can combine its strongest unreleased model with private usage data, internal research problems, specialized evaluators, large compute clusters and a mature agent harness. Better models improve the research system; the research system helps produce better models. Competitors may reproduce individual model weights without reproducing the accumulated machinery that made them.
The verifier is the ceiling
Every loop optimizes a signal. If that signal is incomplete, tests can be overfit, judges persuaded and benchmarks exploited. Models can perform worse after unsupported self-correction, while uncontrolled synthetic data can contribute to model collapse.
The mutable agent must therefore not control its own judge. Evaluators, permission boundaries and held-out tests should sit outside the part of the system allowed to rewrite itself.
This is RSI’s central paradox: the stronger the improver, the stronger and more independent its verifier must become. Current systems work best on measurable problems such as code, mathematics and kernels. Scientific taste, novelty and long-term value remain harder to encode.
Frontier laboratories are therefore no longer building only models. They are building operating systems for research: persistent goals, autonomous loops, experiment environments, agent teams and evaluators. None of this proves that an intelligence explosion has begun. But it does show that the machinery required to test the idea is rapidly being assembled.
The decisive transition will occur when AI stops being merely the product of the laboratory and starts becoming the laboratory itself.
