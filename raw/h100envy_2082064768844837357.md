More context is not better. There is a point past which every extra token makes the answer worse, and it arrives long before the advertised window limit. The mechanics of why, and what to do about it. With code and measurements.
One of the most stubborn myths: the more context you give a model, the better it answers. The reality is the opposite. Chroma's 2025 study ran 18 top models (GPT-4.1, the Claude 4 family, Gemini 2.5, Qwen3) and found every single one answers worse as the input grows. Some held 95 percent accuracy and collapsed to 60 the moment the input crossed a certain length. And the drop is non-linear: the model holds steady, then falls off a cliff, and you cannot predict where.
The discipline that deals with this is called context engineering. It is not about cramming more into the window, but about spending the window wisely. Context is a scarce, biased resource, not a vessel to fill to the brim.
We will cover three things: how the model actually distributes attention across context, why long context degrades because of it, and what specifically to do. With mechanics at the architecture level, not "just write shorter."
Lost in the Middle: Position Matters as Much as Content
Start with the most replicated fact in this area. A model's accuracy depends on where in the context the needed information sits, and the dependence is U-shaped.
Liu et al. (Stanford, TACL 2024) showed: in a multi-document task, accuracy drops by more than 30 percent when the needed document sits in the middle, compared to the beginning or end. This was replicated across six model families and since confirmed on newer architectures. The model sees the beginning (primacy) and the end (recency) well, and goes blind in the middle.
The practical consequence that hits agents: when an agent greps a function name and reads eight files, the needed code in the fourth file lands right in the blind spot. Not because the model is dumb, but because it is in the middle.
This is not a bug that training fixes. It is a property of the decoder-only transformer architecture with softmax attention and positional encoding. Here is why.
Why Context Rots: Three Mechanisms
The term context rot describes quality degradation as the input grows. Three mechanisms sit behind it, and all three are architectural.
First, RoPE decay. Modern models encode position via RoPE (rotary position embeddings), rotating vectors by position. This has a side effect: the dot product between tokens far apart in position systematically decreases (long-term decay, Su et al.). The farther a token, the weaker the attention to it, purely from the geometry of the positional encoding.
Second, softmax concentration. Attention is normalized through softmax, and softmax by nature concentrates mass on the top values. A few high-scoring tokens take almost all the attention, the rest get crumbs. This amplifies primacy and recency: strong positions get stronger, the weak middle gets weaker.
The mechanics are worth seeing in numbers. The attention weight on token j is the softmax over scores: exp(s_j) / Σ exp(s_k), where s_j = q·k_j / √d. The exponential is the key: a difference of a couple of units in scores becomes an order-of-magnitude difference in weights. If one token has a score 3 above the mean, it takes roughly e³ ≈ 20 times more attention than the average one. As the number of tokens grows, the denominator Σ exp(s_k) grows, and for a token in the middle to stay noticeable its score must grow with the logarithm of the context length, which does not happen. Hence a direct consequence: the longer the context, the higher the score threshold needed for a token to be heard at all.
There is a related effect, the attention sink. Models dump excess attention onto the first tokens (often the very first), using them as a "sink" for normalization, even when they are semantically empty. This starves the middle even more: part of the budget goes not to content but to a housekeeping anchor at the start.
Third, attention dilution. Attention is a finite budget. The more tokens, the thinner it spreads. One relevant sentence against a million distractor tokens becomes statistically insignificant. The transformer lets every token attend to every other, which is n² relationships, and as n grows the ability to hold the right relationships stretches to breaking.
The three mechanisms compound. RoPE weakens the distant, softmax starves everything but the peaks, dilution drowns the signal in noise. None is fixed by training, because it is a consequence of how attention is built, not a gap in the data.
Why You Cannot Work Around It at Inference
A temptation arises: since the problem is position, maybe tweak something at inference. Here is why the main attempts fail, useful so you do not waste time.
Raising temperature does not help: temperature scales the logits of the final token distribution, not the internal attention weights. The degradation happens in the attention layers, long before the output softmax.
Reordering tokens helps partially, but hits the KV-cache. In autoregressive generation the keys and values of already-computed tokens are cached with their positional encoding. Position is baked into the cache at the token's first pass, so you cannot "move" a token closer to the end after the fact without recomputation, and recomputation forfeits the cache's whole advantage. Position is not metadata you can edit, it is part of an already-computed representation.
Extending the window by training shifts the ceiling but does not remove the shape of the curve. Models marketed as long-context show the same U-shaped degradation, the cliff just arrives later. RoPE decay and softmax concentration do not disappear because the model was fine-tuned on long sequences.
The hard conclusion: positional degradation is a property of the representation, not a setting. The only reliable lever is not to put in the middle what needs to be heard. Everything else is fighting the architecture on its own field.
The Counterintuitive Part: Structure Hurts
Here is the fact that should change how you think about context. Intuition says a clean, well-structured 100k-token document is easier for the model than the same pile shuffled. The data says the opposite.
Research found that coherent, well-structured input degrades attention more than shuffled input. The mechanism: coherent text spreads attention diffusely across the whole length, while shuffled text creates more distinct local signals the model can latch onto.
The consequence is practical and unpleasant: feeding the model a tidy, well-formatted PDF is not automatically safer than feeding a digest. Structure itself costs accuracy at scale. This does not mean "feed garbage," it means you cannot rely on "but I gave it a clean document" as a guarantee.
The Effective Window Is Much Smaller Than the Advertised One
Another trap. The advertised window size is not the working size. The concept of the Maximum Effective Context Window (MECW): effective context often turns out radically below the advertised, with the gap reaching 99 percent on complex tasks.
That is, a 200k-token window does not mean you have 200k working tokens. On a hard task the truly working ones might be a few thousand, and rot begins past that. Attention degrades non-linearly beyond this invisible ceiling, and context rot begins long before the advertised limit.
The takeaway: do not design for the advertised window. Design for the effective one, and it must be measured, because it depends on the task, not on the number on the box.
What to Do About It: A Budget, Not a Vessel
All of context engineering practice reduces to one shift in thinking. Context is a budget you spend, not a vessel you fill. Every token either helps or dilutes. Four levers.
Lever one: retrieve less. In RAG the temptation to pass the top 20 chunks just in case is directly harmful, they dilute the relevant. Better 3-5 precise chunks after reranking. This is a direct consequence of dilution: every extra chunk is noise that drowns the signal.
Lever two: position on purpose. Since the middle is a blind spot, put the important at the start or the end. Instructions at the start, key data near the end, before the question. Do not scatter the critical across the middle of a long context.
Lever three: compact often. In a long dialogue or agent loop, history accumulates junk from search, attempts, backtracking. That junk degrades every subsequent answer. Periodically summarize history into a compact state and drop the dead turns.
Lever four: isolate per task. Do not drag one growing context through a whole multi-step task. Split into sub-tasks, fresh context for each. This is what stateless iteration does in loop engineering and parallel agents do in a swarm: a short focused context per unit of work.
Code: Measure Your Effective Context
Enough theory, let's measure. The context-rot test is simple: hide a fact (a needle) in the context at varying depth and varying total length, check whether the model finds it. This is needle-in-a-haystack, but focused on position and length, not only on the fact of finding.
python
import random

def build_haystack(needle: str, filler_sentences: list[str],
                   total_tokens: int, needle_depth: float) -> str:
    """Builds a context of a given length with the needle at a given depth.
    needle_depth: 0.0 start, 0.5 middle, 1.0 end."""
    # rough estimate: 1 sentence ~ 20 tokens
    n_fillers = total_tokens // 20
    body = [random.choice(filler_sentences) for _ in range(n_fillers)]
    insert_at = int(len(body) * needle_depth)
    body.insert(insert_at, needle)
    return " ".join(body)

def probe(ask, needle_fact: str, question: str,
          filler: list[str], lengths: list[int], depths: list[float]):
    """Runs a length x depth grid, returns where the model loses the needle."""
    grid = {}
    for L in lengths:
        for d in depths:
            ctx = build_haystack(needle_fact, filler, L, d)
            prompt = f"{ctx}\
\
Question: {question}"
            answer = ask("Answer only from the text above.", prompt)
            hit = needle_fact.split()[-1].lower() in answer.lower()
            grid[(L, d)] = hit
    return grid

def report(grid, lengths, depths):
    print("len \\ depth:", "  ".join(f"{d:.1f}" for d in depths))
    for L in lengths:
        row = "  ".join(" OK " if grid[(L, d)] else "MISS" for d in depths)
        print(f"{L:>7}      {row}")
Run this on your model with real filler from your domain, and you see your own rot map: at what length and depth the model starts losing the needle. The picture is usually exactly as in theory: the middle falls off first, and the longer the context, the wider the blind spot. This is your personal MECW, measured, not taken from the box.
The key point: test on your own domain. The effective window depends on how similar your distractors are to the answer. The harder it is to tell the needed from the surrounding text, the faster accuracy falls, so other people's benchmarks do not transfer to your task.
A Metric: One Number Instead of a Grid
An OK/MISS grid is vivid, but tracking regressions needs a single number. A model's positional sensitivity on your domain can be reduced to a coefficient: how much accuracy drops in the middle relative to the edges.
python
def positional_sensitivity(grid, lengths, depths) -> dict:
    """Computes the middle's dropoff relative to the edges, per length.
    Returns the drop fraction: 0.0 no dropoff, 1.0 full middle failure."""
    edge_depths = [d for d in depths if d <= 0.15 or d >= 0.85]
    mid_depths  = [d for d in depths if 0.35 <= d <= 0.65]
    out = {}
    for L in lengths:
        edge_hits = sum(grid[(L, d)] for d in edge_depths)
        mid_hits  = sum(grid[(L, d)] for d in mid_depths)
        edge_rate = edge_hits / max(len(edge_depths), 1)
        mid_rate  = mid_hits  / max(len(mid_depths), 1)
        # drop = how much worse the middle is than the edges, normalized to edges
        drop = (edge_rate - mid_rate) / max(edge_rate, 1e-9)
        out[L] = round(max(drop, 0.0), 2)
    return out

# example output: {500: 0.0, 2000: 0.5, 8000: 1.0}
# at 8000 tokens the middle is fully blind, the edges hold
This number is worth running in CI on your eval when the model or version changes. If positional_sensitivity at your working length went up, the new model holds the middle worse, and you want to know that before it surfaces in production. One metric catches a regression that a grid shows but does not compare.
Estimating the Effective Length
The second useful number is where exactly the cliff begins. Not "the model degrades," but "on this domain the cliff is around N tokens."
python
def effective_length(grid, lengths, depths, threshold=0.5) -> int:
    """The largest length at which the average accuracy across all depths
    still holds above the threshold. This is the practical MECW."""
    best = 0
    for L in sorted(lengths):
        acc = sum(grid[(L, d)] for d in depths) / len(depths)
        if acc >= threshold:
            best = L
        else:
            break
    return best

# on this domain the model is reliable up to about effective_length tokens,
# beyond that design for splitting, not for long context
These two numbers, positional_sensitivity and effective_length, turn the vague "context rots" into two figures on your domain that you can track and design against.
Code: A Context Budget With Priority
Now a practical context builder that respects the budget and position. It takes chunks by priority, fits the token budget, and places the important at the edges, not the middle.
python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Chunk:
    text: str
    priority: int          # higher = more important
    tokens: int

def build_context(chunks: list[Chunk], budget_tokens: int,
                  question: str) -> str:
    """Fits chunks into the budget and lays them at the edges (anti lost-in-middle)."""
    # 1. sort by priority, take while it fits the budget
    ordered = sorted(chunks, key=lambda c: c.priority, reverse=True)
    selected, used = [], 0
    for c in ordered:
        if used + c.tokens > budget_tokens:
            continue
        selected.append(c)
        used += c.tokens

    # 2. lay out: most important at start and end, weak ones in the middle
    selected.sort(key=lambda c: c.priority, reverse=True)
    head, tail, middle = [], [], []
    for i, c in enumerate(selected):
        if i % 3 == 0:
            head.append(c)      # top priorities to the start
        elif i % 3 == 1:
            tail.append(c)      # next ones to the end
        else:
            middle.append(c)    # least important to the blind middle
    layout = head + middle + list(reversed(tail))

    body = "\
\
".join(c.text for c in layout)
    # question at the very end: recency position, best attention
    return f"{body}\
\
Question: {question}"
Two ideas in the code. Selection by budget, not by "will it fit the window": we aim at the effective context, not the advertised one, so budget_tokens is set well below the window size. And edge layout: the important goes into the primacy and recency zones, the least important is deliberately exiled to the middle, where the blind spot is, because if anything is to be lost, let it be the least needed.
When Long Context Is Justified After All
So as not to fall into the dogma of "always cut." There are tasks where long context is justified, and the 2026 practical default is hybrid: retrieve 50-200k relevant tokens, then reason with long context over them.
The difference is that you do not dump everything, you first narrow with retrieval to the relevant, and only the selected goes into the long window. Long context is a tool for reasoning over the already-filtered, not a dump for everything at hand. Retrieval did not die as windows grew, it became the first filter before long context.
Final
Context is not a vessel but a budget with a biased attention distribution. The model sees the beginning and end well, goes blind in the middle, and this is an architectural property of RoPE plus softmax plus dilution that training does not fix. The effective window is radically smaller than the advertised, structure can hurt more than chaos, and more tokens often means a worse answer.
The practice that follows is one: spend the budget deliberately. Retrieve less, position the important at the edges, compact history, isolate sub-tasks. And measure your effective context with the code above, rather than trusting the number on the box. Whoever fills the window degrades the model. Whoever spends the budget keeps it sharp.
