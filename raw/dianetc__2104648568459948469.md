## Linked article

Fetched from https://dianetc.com/musings/jev/ — "Jev, Regression, and More" by Diane (Sep 2026). The post above is a short pointer to this page, so the substance is summarized here with its numbers.

Diane tests Jev zero-shot on two **reasoning-intensive regression (RiR)** tasks from the RiR paper (arXiv:2508.21762): natural-language regression where a numeric answer can only be produced by reasoning over the input, not by reading off surface features. She places RiR at Level 3 of a three-level hierarchy — Level 1 predicts from supplied features, Level 2 needs shallow semantic understanding, Level 3 requires working through the content before any number can be assigned.

**Wrappers, because Jev emits no raw numbers.** For math error detection (predict the first erroneous step of a solution on a 0–10 scale), each solution is split into roughly equal character intervals and Jev answers a *Choice* question about which interval holds the first wrong step; intervals map to midpoints and the prediction is the probability-weighted average. For pairwise RAG comparison (−2 to +2 quality difference), she uses a *Score* question with five levels from "substantially worse" to "substantially better", plus a separate finer-grained *Choice* grid.

**Results** (CCC higher is better; NMSE lower is better, 1.0 = always predicting the mean):

| Method | CCC | NMSE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Math error — Jev Choice | 0.65 | 0.49 |
| Math error — GPT-5 detailed prompt | 0.69 | 0.78 |
| Math error — MENTAT (GPT-5) | 0.78 | 0.42 |
| Pairwise RAG — Jev Score | 0.39 | 1.35 |
| Pairwise RAG — Jev Choice | 0.35 | 1.33 |
| Pairwise RAG — GPT-4.1 detailed prompt | 0.47 | 2.20 |
| Pairwise RAG — GPT-5 detailed prompt | 0.31 | 2.18 |
| Pairwise RAG — MENTAT (GPT-4.1) | 0.52 | 0.80 |

On math error detection Jev nearly matches detailed GPT-5 prompting (CCC 0.65 vs 0.69) and beats it by 37% on NMSE — but she argues the NMSE gap reflects shrinkage, not better error-finding: averaging over probabilities pulls predictions toward the middle when the model is unsure, and squared error rewards exactly that. Jev's predictions span at most ~63% of the targets' spread while its correlation with the truth (~0.7) is similar to the baselines'. A simple post-hoc calibration (shift plus rescaling, fit on training examples and selected on a separate dev set) raises math-error CCC to about 0.70 on the same 750 test examples.

On pairwise RAG, Jev lands between the paper's two LLMs — above every GPT-5 configuration, below every GPT-4.1 one — with the best NMSE of any zero-shot method, though still above 1.0, i.e. worse than predicting the mean. Working backward from GPT-4.1's metrics, its correlation with human scores is at least 0.55 versus 0.41 for Jev, so GPT-4.1 carries more real signal and merely spreads its guesses too wide; the finer Choice grid did not help beyond run-to-run noise.

Her closing argument: there is real interest in small, cheap models for classification, and the same likely holds for regression — a "foundation model for regression". She names TabPFN and time-series forecasters as earlier numerical-prediction foundation models, but says the interesting distinction is the kind of work required to reach the prediction, and cites REAL (arXiv:2603.17145) on regression-aware LLM judging as related work.
