Rieseberg (an engineering lead on Claude, Electron co-maintainer) writes that model capability "doesn't grow uniformly" — quoting Terence Tao: models are "astounding in some directions, while inadequate in others". Most users stay at "what is the capital of France"; a few "pilot the astounding capabilities and seem to be flying". He splits ambition into four types, and explicitly skips **Speed** (same work, less time) as already commoditized.

**Altitude** — climb the abstraction ladder from task to outcome. Instead of asking for individual crash fixes, he eventually asked Claude to "simply keep our apps crash-free". Worked example on a quarterly business review: (1) feed the model all available data and ask "what changed this quarter, why, and what should we do about it"; (2) ask it to work out the three most urgent decisions and draft for/against arguments; (3) ask it to design a better mechanism than a quarterly deck, prove from company history that it would have caught problems sooner, and run it in parallel for a quarter. He notes altitude requires workflow changes — crash-free apps needed crash-report access, a version↔git-commit map, and Windows/macOS/Linux machines so the model can reproduce and verify fixes. Limits remain: "'Claude, go make money' doesn't seem to work for most businesses by itself in 2026".

**Depth** — use the instruments of the best professionals, not their vocabulary. "When Claude reaches for markdown tables, insist on the use of databases and statistical models." Techniques: pick unreasonable goals and assert they are reasonable (200ms instead of 5s); give raw data and adversarially re-test assumptions ("We believe churn is an onboarding problem. Try to reproduce that from the raw data."); produce new knowledge ("Build a simulation of our customers that reproduces the last year from our logs. Then find the earliest signal of churn we don't know about yet, and show it predicts on data it has not seen."); and never let the model judge itself — insist on outside validation, ideally a tool it can run. His reusable prompt asks the model to (1) say what a top professional would do that you are not asking for, (2) name the rawest material they would start from, (3) name their specific methods and tools, (4) say what other than you or the model would show the result is right — then rewrite the prompt.

**Range** — pull in other fields, increasingly economical with multi-agent setups. He cites Alex Albert rendering interior designs in Blender as the trigger for his own 3D homepage. Two techniques: replace nouns with verbs (from *The Art of Innovation*: a QBR is not "a deck", it is "getting busy people to believe a story about cause and effect, and to decide under uncertainty, in 30 minutes"); and "assemble the crew" — analyst, journalist, editor, motion/audio designer, plus a red team (short seller, forensic accountant). Caveat: "Don't ask the model to *write* like a short seller, ask it to *do* what a short seller *does*." All of this assumes the model has a computer with software-install rights and data access (MCP connectors).

The closing section argues the constraint is self-efficacy, not capability. He cites Locke and Latham's 35 years of goal-setting research (specific hard goals beat "do your best"), Patrick Collison's "Fast" list, George Dantzig copying two unsolved statistics problems off a blackboard in 1939 thinking they were homework, Roger Bannister's 1954 four-minute mile (sole holder for only 46 days), Sabastian Sawe's sub-two-hour marathon in London in April with the runner-up also under two hours, *The Inner Game of Tennis*, and Linux's "just a hobby" announcement.
