95% of enterprise AI projects produce no measurable return. Not because the models are bad. Because nobody could make them survive contact with a real company. 
That single number is the whole reason this job exists, and the reason it pays what it does. An MIT NANDA study analyzed over 300 enterprise AI deployments and found that only about 5 percent produced real operational or financial impact. The other 95% stalled, after an estimated $30-40 billion in spend. 

The models worked in the demo. The deployments died on the way to production: they could not talk to a legacy database, could not pass a compliance review, could not survive being handed to the ops team that inherited them.
There is a role built specifically to close that gap, and it is the best-paid non-research engineering job in AI right now.
Total comp runs from around $215K at Palantir's median to north of $785K for senior engineers at Anthropic and OpenAI. 
Nobody in that role is training a model. They walk into a business with legacy systems, a compliance department, and a skeptical ops team, and they come out weeks later with something that runs.
This is not a job description. It is a go-to-market strategy, and the people executing it get paid like it. This is the 8-step roadmap into it: what the job actually is, the stack that gets you in, and the interview round that eliminates most people who already passed the coding.
01. Learn what the job actually is
A Forward Deployed Engineer is the engineer the company sends to the customer. Not for a sales call. For weeks at a time, sitting next to the people who will use the product, learning their workflow in painful detail, and shipping custom code that makes their version of it work.
The best mental model is not consultant and it is not solutions engineer. It is founding engineer, working on someone else's product. There is no PM in the room to set scope, no staff engineer to escalate the architecture to, no backlog telling you what matters. 
You decide what to build, what to fake, and what to push back on, in the room, that week.
The rhythm is consistent across the top AI companies: an FDE embeds with a customer for a few weeks, ships something that works, and then the core engineering org slowly productizes whatever turned out to be general. That loop is the strategic point of the role. You are delivering revenue and doing the company's highest-fidelity product research at the same time.
02. Understand why the role exploded
That 95% number is the foundation of the entire career path, so it is worth being precise about why those projects failed. Not on capability. On integration: systems that could not talk to legacy SQL, could not handle the customer's authentication, could not meet data residency rules, could not be maintained by the team that inherited them.
Three forces then pointed the same way at once. AI broke generic SaaS, because every enterprise has unique data, unique workflows, and its own definition of good enough, so selling AI to a large company is now always also selling an integration project. 

Labs need to deploy at the speed the technology moves, and a six-month integration kills a pilot before it lands. And AI tooling made the economics work: with the modern stack, one strong FDE does what took a small team a few years ago, which is the only reason flying a human onsite for weeks pencils out.
The bottleneck in AI is no longer capability. It is deployment, and the market is repricing accordingly.
03. Map the market and pick target
The title is unstable, and that is the first practical thing to know. Searching only for "Forward Deployed Engineer" hides most of the market. The same job ships as Applied AI Engineer, which is Anthropic's name for it, as Forward Deployed Software Engineer or FDSE, which is Palantir's, and as Solutions Engineer, Deployment Engineer, or Founding Engineer (Customer-Facing) at smaller startups. Search all of them.
Compensation splits sharply by tier, and the split is almost entirely equity. Palantir's median total comp sits around . Frontier labs run a different market: 
mid-level total comp lands around $385K, staff around $610K, and senior FDEs at Anthropic and OpenAI reach north of $785K, with principal levels clearing seven figures. 
At the top of the market, equity is 55 to 70 percent of the package, which means the headline base a recruiter quotes understates the real offer. Anthropic's own public listings put base around $200K-$300K, before the equity that does most of the work.
One planning note worth having early: read the offer as total comp, not base, because the base is the smallest true part of it at a frontier lab.
04. Build engineering breadth, not depth
This is the counterintuitive part, and it is where specialists get it wrong. The strongest FDEs are not the deepest engineers their company has. They are the ones who can hold six domains in their head at once and switch between them cheaply. Depth in one area is worth less here than being genuinely solid across all of it.
The floor looks like this. Python and TypeScript cover most of what you will touch. One cloud, whichever your target customers actually run. One database you can debug under pressure, and one frontend framework you can throw a working interface together in. 

That is enough to start. You do not need to be the best engineer in the room. You need to be the only one who can do all of it.
Two softer layers matter as much, and they are what the interview actually probes.

# Rate yourself 1-5. Anything below 3 is your next month.

## Engineering breadth            # solid, not elite
[ ] Python           - backend, scripts, data glue
[ ] TypeScript       - integrations and a usable frontend

[ ] One database     - can debug it under pressure
[ ] One UI framework - stand up a working interface in a day

## AI-native                      # ship, don't train
[ ] Prompt engineering beyond trial and error
[ ] Model APIs: streaming, tool use, token budgets
[ ] RAG - and knowing when retrieval is the WRONG answer
[ ] Structured outputs and schema validation
[ ] Evals - the layer that separates demo from production
[ ] One agent framework, something actually built

## The half nobody practices
[ ] Ran a workshop with a non-technical stakeholder
[ ] Said "we should not build that" to a paying customer
[ ] Expressed your work in dollars or hours saved
[ ] Learned an unfamiliar industry well enough to ship in it
Product judgment, because you are the PM in the room. And business acumen, because someone will ask what the ROI is and will need the answer to defend the project to their own leadership. 

Framing your work in dollars and hours saved is a real, learnable skill most engineers never practice.
05. Learn to ship AI, not train it
Here is the single most common misconception standing between engineers and this job: they assume they need to train models. You do not. Nobody is asking an FDE to fine-tune anything. You need to ship them, which is a completely different and far more learnable skill set.
Weight your preparation toward evals and failure modes, because that is what enterprise deployment is actually made of: debugging hallucinations, retrieval failures, bad tool calls, brittle multi-step workflows, and reasoning about latency, cost, reliability, and security as tradeoffs rather than checkboxes.
Anyone can get a demo working. The entire value of an FDE is being the person who knows why the demo will not survive contact with a real company, and what to do about it.
06. Build the three artifacts enterprises actually buy
This is where a generic "learn AI" roadmap stops being useful. Anthropic's own FDE postings name the deliverables plainly: you embed with customers to build production applications with Claude, and you ship MCP servers, sub-agents, and agent skills. Those three artifacts are the concrete unit of work.
MCP servers are the integration layer, the thing that wires Claude into the customer's actual systems: their ticketing, their warehouse, their internal API with no docs and one person who understands it. 

Agent skills encode the customer's specific workflow so Claude follows their process instead of a generic one. Sub-agents handle the work that would otherwise blow out a context window on a long task.
Build one of each against a real, messy system and you have what almost no candidate has: a portfolio of exactly the artifacts the job produces

07. Ship one real deployment for a real user
Every FDE posting screens for the same phrase in some form: shipped production AI systems. Not studied, not prototyped. Shipped, to someone who depended on it. 

That is the wall most candidates hit, and it is the one thing on this list you cannot read your way past.
So manufacture the experience deliberately. Find one real workflow belonging to someone who is not you: a small business, a nonprofit, a team inside your current company. Sit with them. Watch them work. Build the thing that removes the worst part of their week, deploy it where they actually use it, and stay long enough to fix what breaks. 
That last part is not optional. The whole job is what happens after the demo.
Then write it up the way an FDE reports, because the write-up is half the artifact.

08. Run the discovery round, and the loop around it
This is the step to read twice. The interview loop is unusually consistent across the top companies, and it is built to filter in both directions at once: against pure algorithmic engineers who cannot communicate, and against smooth consultants who cannot code.
Expect roughly five stages. A recruiter screen on motivation and level. A technical use-case screen, which at Anthropic is a practical scenario around deploying Claude with MCP tooling, where you plan and execute a long-running task and reason about reliability and context-window management. 
A coding round that is practical rather than LeetCode: a rate limiter, streaming data, a job queue, a token-budget allocator, often with new customer constraints layered in mid-exercise to see whether you refactor cleanly. 
A hiring manager round on past projects. And a final panel on solution design and values.
The one that catches strong engineers is the customer conversation. By multiple accounts, a large share of candidates who clear the coding rounds are eliminated at this one, because they hear a customer problem and start solving it. They propose an architecture. Some open an editor. The candidates who advance do the opposite: they run it like a research interview.
python
# Run the round like a researcher. Solving too early is the tell.

## Surface the real constraints
- What have you already tried, and why did it stop?
  # failed deployments hide every constraint that matters
- What cannot change no matter what we build?
  # compliance, latency, data residency, the union contract
- Who has to approve this, and what will they object to?

## Find the actual workflow
- Walk me through the last time this went wrong.
- Who does this today, and what does it cost them per week?
- What happens downstream if we get it wrong at 3am?

## Define "good enough" - theirs, not yours
- What accuracy would make you turn this off?
- How will you know in 90 days whether this worked?

## Close the loop
- Reflect back what you heard. Get it corrected.
- Name what you would NOT build, and why.
  # "we shouldn't build that" is a senior signal, not a dodge
Two preparation notes people skip. Mission alignment is screened seriously at Anthropic: read the Core Views on AI Safety and the Responsible Scaling Policy before you apply, because generic enthusiasm does not pass. And the postings ask for calibrated judgment about model risks, which quietly eliminates otherwise strong candidates. Being able to say clearly where you would not deploy a model, and why, is part of the bar.
Six starting points - find yours
You have production experience, no AI layer. Your instincts are the scarce half; most AI-native candidates have never run anything real. Add prompting, model APIs, structured outputs, and evals, then ship one MCP server against a messy internal system.
You come from ML or research. You are overqualified on modeling and underqualified on everything else. The job is integration, constraints, and stakeholders. Deliberately build something boring that survives a compliance review and an ops handoff.
You want the frontier labs. Those roles are rarely entry-level. Aim one ring out, at the startups and consultancies deploying AI into enterprises, do the same work with less gatekeeping, and move up in two years.
You can talk but not yet ship. The loop is explicitly designed to filter out smooth talkers who cannot code. Your entire preparation is one shipped, maintained, publicly documented system.
You can ship but freeze in the room. Discovery is the round that eliminates strong engineers. Practice it in the exact same sessions from step 7: every time you sit with the person whose workflow you are fixing, you are rehearsing it.
You may be doing this already. Internal platform engineers who sit with business units are running the FDE motion under a different title. Rewrite your experience in the role's language, workflows changed and hours saved and constraints navigated, and you are a live candidate today.
Conclusion: the scarce thing was never the model. It's the person who can land it
Capability stopped being the bottleneck somewhere in the last two years. What is scarce now is the engineer who can walk into a company with legacy systems, a compliance department, 
and a skeptical ops team, and come out weeks later with something that actually runs.
That is a strange combination of skills, which is exactly why it pays like it does. Breadth over depth. Judgment with no PM to lean on. The patience to sit in someone else's messy reality long enough to understand it before writing a line of code.
Most engineers will keep optimizing for the roles that existed in 2020. The ones who learn to deploy will own the decade, because every model that ships from here still has to survive contact with a real company, and someone has to be the one who makes it.
