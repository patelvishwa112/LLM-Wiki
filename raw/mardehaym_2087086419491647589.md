If you can describe what your team does, step by step, exceptions included, you're ahead of most companies trying to deploy an agent right now.
Low bar. Most companies can't clear it.
Boardroom conversations fixate on models. Which one. How many parameters. Whose benchmark improved this week. 
I'd put model selection at maybe 5% of whether an AI project survives. The other 95% is whether the company has a process worth automating.
The gap between companies that documented their workflows and companies that didn't existed before agents showed up. Agents made it visible and expensive.

For context, I run @LimestoneHQ. We do AI transformation for PE-backed enterprises, embedding engineering teams inside client orgs for months, sometimes years. We sit inside the workflows that run between the org chart boxes. After hundreds of client engagements, the pattern is the same: process maturity determines the outcome.
The hours that didn't add up
One of our long-standing clients runs a time-tracking workflow. Engineers log hours, some months come in light with vacations and holidays, other months spike above forecast. When billable hours exceed the year-to-date average, the client calls and asks why.
Simple question. Reasonable concern. The kind of exchange you'd think an agent could handle in a weekend.
Before you automate it, you need four things: a forecast of expected hours per period, year-to-date actuals as a running average, a rule for when actuals exceed forecast by X% that triggers a client-facing explanation, and a delivery mechanism.
A 3-step agent. Maybe a week to build.
But steps 1 through 3 didn't exist anywhere. Someone kept the forecast in their head. Account managers passed the "rule" to each other over lunch as tribal knowledge. A different person wrote the explanation each month, and no two versions matched.
So we documented a process instead of building an agent. Three weeks. The agent build took one.
That 3:1 ratio, documentation to build, is typical. Nobody wants to hear it. For it to work, every use case requires understanding, analysis, a thought-through workflow, the desire to sit down and do it. 
Not once, but continuously.

What skipping documentation costs you
"The demo worked." That's the most irrelevant sentence in enterprise AI.
When you deploy an agent against an undocumented process, the agent doesn't pause and ask questions. It fills the gaps with assumptions about handoff points, decision criteria, and escalation rules.
I've watched this happen at three clients in the past year. The agent builds a workflow from whatever data it can find in the CRM, email templates, Slack message patterns. It constructs something that looks plausible on a demo screen but misses the judgment calls, the edge cases, the "call the client first if it's over $50K" rule that one person carries in their head.
Three weeks later you're fielding calls from clients who got the wrong email, or discovering that the agent routed a $200K deal to a junior rep because the ownership rules in Salesforce hadn't been updated since 2021.
Documentation means writing down what your team runs today. The version they follow, including the part where someone checks a spreadsheet that doesn't connect to anything and sends a manual email because an integration broke in 2019 and nobody fixed it.
Three weeks for a 10-person team's core workflow is normal. Companies skip this because it's tedious and because "we'll figure it out during implementation" feels faster. An agent configured during implementation inherits assumptions about edge cases the implementation team never asked about.
Documentation also forces decisions. When you write down "if variance exceeds 10%, auto-send standard language," you've made a business decision. Before that sentence exists, three account managers handle the same variance three different ways and an agent can't choose between them.
Someone has to pick one and commit it to a document. That decision-making is the part companies try to skip, and the part that determines whether the agent runs or breaks.

Why coding agents went vertical
Coding agents work because software engineering is the most documented, structured, observable workflow in a company. 
Developers share a common language (code), common tools (Git, CI/CD, IDEs), and common metrics. 
Software engineering is also continuous, digital, and unbounded. Economic value maps to digital output, and task size can grow without limit in a single session. As models improve and agents handle larger workloads, you scale deployment with no ramp because the work is end-to-end digital.
Most enterprise work doesn't have this property. A sales rep needs a feedback loop with the customer before doing additional work on a specific account. A lawyer needs to talk to the client. A doctor needs to interact with the patient. These domains hit a wall that coding never hits: a human has to respond before the agent can continue.
Matan Grinberg at Factory put it well on the Training Data podcast: if everyone called in sick tomorrow, token usage would plummet. Nobody would be there to prompt the agents. Right? That tells you how early we are.
In legal, agents should process every contract that comes in. In sales, agents should roam through customer records and surface signals for when to do outreach. In life sciences, swarms of agents should read through every test result and research paper.
To get there, you can't bolt agents onto a 15-step manual process. You reengineer that process into 5 steps. AI handles 4. But the reengineering requires documented processes as the starting point, and most companies don't have them.
Sidekicks vs. background agents
The enterprise value ratio between AI sidekicks and AI background agents is 1 to 10.
AI sidekicks require you to prompt them. They handle tasks on the fly and help you move faster through your work, but they don't take work off your plate. Sidekicks bring 10-20% efficiency for the average desk worker, and can cost a fortune if mismanaged.
AI background agents run on their own. They surface for exceptions, judgments, and other human-in-the-loop events. They remove work from your plate, and the workflows we take on see 60-90% efficiency gains.
Production-ready thing versus prompting your way into it is life and day.
The market is full of sidekicks: Claude Cowork, Codex, GitHub Copilot. Almost zero background agents exist in production.
Building a background agent means going deep into a company and mapping how processes run today, including how exceptions get handled. You reengineer those processes for an AI-native future. You build agents custom for this process on top of the systems of record, so if your work runs on Dynamics 365 and Salesforce, your agents run there too. Then you integrate human-in-the-loop feedback and model optimization so the agents get smarter, faster, and cheaper over time.
I mean, this is the entire process-first argument compressed into one problem. Background agents can't run without documented, reengineered processes. And almost nobody has them.

The fix is boring
Step 1. Write down your top 10 workflows. The way they work today, exceptions and workarounds included, including the part where someone says "ask Janet, she knows." If you can't describe it in steps, you can't automate it.
Step 2. Connect agents to the system of record. Salesforce, NetSuite, SAP, Dynamics. Agents that survive production live inside the system where data exists and work gets done.
Step 3. Build observability. Once agents run, you need tracing, logging, and telemetry. What the agent decided, why, what it got wrong. The industry is standardizing on OpenTelemetry for this. Arthur AI codified it as the Agent Development Lifecycle: observability surfaces failure modes, evaluation suites capture them as test cases, policy updates prevent recurrence. Celonis runs Agent Mining from the process side.
Companies that completed step 1 can build an agent that monitors other agents and suggests process improvements. A self-improvement loop. Companies that skipped step 1 can't.
The sequence matters. You can't observe what you haven't deployed, and you can't deploy what you haven't documented.
FIGURE 7 — Document → Connect → Observe
A client has account managers tracking billable hours across multiple projects. Some accounts spike above forecast each month. Old process: someone spots the anomaly in a spreadsheet, writes a custom explanation email, sends it for internal review, gets sign-off, forwards to client. About 45 minutes per account, 30 flagged accounts per month, 22.5 hours burned on one task.
We documented three tiers. Auto-send for variances under 10% with standard language. Draft-and-review for 10-25%. Escalate to account manager above 25%. Agent connects to the time system, pulls forecast, calculates variance, generates the right response tier, routes it.
22.5 hours became 3 hours of review. The agent build took one week. Process documentation took three.
The 3:1 ratio again.
An agent without a documented process has nothing to execute. An agent with a broken process breaks it faster.
Stop reporting AI adoption to your leadership team. Seat count, login count, prompt volume. Organizations measure activity, not outcomes. Your pilot count is not a KPI.
Report this instead: what percentage of your core business workflows are documented, connected to a system of record, and observable?
That's your automation readiness score. The rest is theater.
