## Linked article

**Source:** Andrej Karpathy, "Doing well in your courses — a guide" (https://cs.stanford.edu/people/karpathy/advice.html). The Stanford host refused connections at ingest time, so the text was read from the Internet Archive snapshot dated 2026-10-15.

The page is a list of rules of thumb for undergraduates, organised as general habits, test preparation, the day of the test, during the test, and a closing argument about what actually matters.

- Sleep ~7.5 hours (4 hours absolute minimum); all-nighters are "not worth it" because the brain consolidates material overnight. He starts studying days ahead partly to buy more nights.
- Before studying, write every topic out as bullets and estimate how long each takes, otherwise the early material eats the time and the important later material gets skimmed.
- Look at past exams before studying, especially from the same professor — note the question types, don't attempt them yet.
- Reading/understanding is not the same as reproducing: close the book and rewrite the derivation, or you will often find you can't.
- Study alone first, collaborate near the end; don't only study with stronger students, since explaining to weaker ones teaches you.
- Go to office hours at least once before a final, even with a made-up question; professors sometimes say more one-on-one.
- Time budget he suggests: ~3 days for midterms, ~6 days for exams.
- For math, exercises beat reading; write a cheat sheet even if you can't bring it.
- Study in libraries or reading rooms with other people working; noisy places have a documented negative effect on learning.
- Test day: coffee and food at T-2 hours (coffee immediately before is "always bad"), study intensely until 30-45 minutes before.
- In the test: pencil, a 1-3 second glance at every question up front, easy questions first (he has completed as little as 30% on a first pass), box the answer, stay neat because a human marks it, use the point value of each question as a signal, and never leave early.
- Closing point, which he says he wishes someone had told him as an undergrad: "no one will care about your grades, unless they are bad." His rule of thumb is that the smartest student gets 85% in every course — roughly a 4.0 without over- or under-studying — and spends the saved time on real-world experience: a summer internship, research with a professor (whose reference letter "completely dwarfs" grades), at least one paper, and open-source or side projects documented publicly.
- Corollary warning: don't be the undergrad who joins a research project, asks a lot of questions, then disappears — it damages your reputation.
