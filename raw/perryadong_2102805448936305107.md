## Linked article

Fetched from https://pd-perry.github.io/posts/post-training.html (Perry Dong with Chelsea Finn, September 2026, 13 min read). The t.co link in the post resolves to this blog post.

**Thesis.** Pretrained robotics models in 2026 look like LLMs in the GPT-2 era: Physical Intelligence, Generalist and DeepMind have shown VLAs and world-action models (WAMs) executing genuinely complex tasks. But complex behavior is not reliability — a robot that loads dishes correctly 95% of the time breaks something every week in a house with glass, pets and kids. What is missing is the second half of the LLM playbook: post-training. "RL shows incredible promise in training frontier robotics models, but **it is not yet a recipe. It's a craft.**"

**The LLM recipe they want to replicate:** (1) start from a strong pretrained model; (2) define environments and reward (verifiable or learned preference models); (3) run RL with a specific algorithm family anchored to a reference model; (4) watch for known pathologies such as reward hacking.

**Why robotics RL is a different problem.** The RL that scaled for Go and LLMs is on-policy policy gradient with cheap samples and cheap verification — AlphaGo's illustrated example plays 288 games in parallel and scores 3,168 games. Robotics breaks every assumption: a single "pick up a glass" episode is ~500 control steps with a 7-D action emitted every 20 ms, and reward arrives only at step 500, so credit must be assigned across hundreds of decisions. Real-world samples are expensive and simulation of real objects can be harder than learning the task. The environment is stochastic, so the same command twice produces different behavior. That pushes the field toward value-based RL — which has been validated at scale far less than LLM post-training.

**Why DDPG/TD3/SAC don't transfer.** They assume a unimodal Gaussian action distribution, but frontier policies use diffusion-style action generation because many correct actions exist (grasp a cup by the handle or the base). Sending gradients back through the whole denoising chain is unstable; scoring candidate actions with a value function and executing the best one is safe but leaves performance on the table because the policy never learns; RL-searching input noise is low-risk but can only recombine behaviors the base model already has.

**EXPO(-FT).** Their system learns a lightweight edit policy that makes small, bounded nudges to the frontier model's proposed actions toward higher-value regions, scores candidates with a value function, and then absorbs the accepted edits back into the base model so it proposes better actions next time. Human interventions during online learning are fed back into training. Result: **30/30 success across six manipulation tasks (string-light routing, pool shots, flower insertion, egg flipping, ball balancing) using an average of ~19 minutes of online interaction**, versus averages of 18.8 for SFT on π0.5, 20.5 for HG-DAgger, 19 for DSRL and 5.5 for HIL-SERL.

**Stated limits:** humans still define success, reset the scene and intervene; longer horizons strain the value function (they tested the shorter-horizon end); and 19 minutes of robot interaction is not 19 minutes of training — gradient updates on a model this large dominate wall-clock time.

**The protocol gap — five unspecified defaults:** reward specification (LLM RLVR has "check the answer, check the tests"; robotics has hand-built success detectors or a human calling each trajectory), resets (learned reset policies or reversible task design), human-in-the-loop policy, hyperparameter tuning (learning rate, update-to-data ratio, stopping point, task horizon, control frequency), and initialization (how much data, what it contains, how to weight it against fresh online experience).
