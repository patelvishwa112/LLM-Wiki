1. Instead of asking people what they need, spend your time listening to how they talk about their work. If you pay attention to the recurring friction in meetings, emails, and chat logs, you start to see the real problems.

2. When someone asks for a specific fix, treat it as a symptom, not a diagnosis. Look at their actual workflow to see if they are solving a real blocker or just fighting a fire you could prevent. Talk to system owners and people who touch multiple parts of the stack; they usually have the clearest view of where things break.

3. Resist the urge to fix everything the moment you hear about it. Quick reactions usually just patch surface-level issues, leaving the underlying problem untouched. Wait for a few similar requests to overlap. Once you see a pattern, treat it like a hypothesis.

4. Before you start coding, write up a proposal or a quick prototype to see if your fix actually clears the bottleneck. If a problem is messy, don't try to build a single, perfect system to handle it. Break it into smaller, specialized pieces that fit the way people work.

5. When it comes time to build, pick your path based on the risk. If a fix is easy and helpful, just do it. If you are not sure it will work, write code you can throw away to test your assumptions. If a project is high-stakes, get the team to agree on the plan through a standard review process first.

6. Your goal is to shape problems in a way that lets others take the lead on the technical execution. As you solve these issues, you build trust across the organization, which eventually gives you better insight into the next set of problems that need solving.
