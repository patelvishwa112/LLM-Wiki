[Neural networks are not already clean](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/Dkh7pXKjoNWkkxQDz/neural-networks-are-not-already-clean)
by leogao, 16 Oct 2026 (~5 min read).

Leo Gao responds to Nick Cammarata's "already clean" post, which argued that neurons
in fact often have a simple pattern behind their activating examples, and that the field
was too quick to write neurons off as polysemantic. Gao grants the point — the historical
polysemanticity estimate was too high and people should update — but argues it is "nowhere
near a sufficient argument that we understand most of what neurons are doing," and does not
show interpretability is solved or vastly easier.

**What it takes to explain a neuron.** Two failure modes of the "vibes check" on activating
examples:

1. *The pattern need not be necessary.* Every example that activates a neuron fitting pattern
   X does not mean every X-activating input fires the neuron ("X matches Y is not commutative").
   The illustrative case: top activations look like "dogs riding bicycles," the 20th percentile
   looks like a dog near a bicycle, weak activations are still dog sentences — but the neuron
   may in fact only fire on dogs on bicycles *on sunny days*. Gao says this was a big problem
   for the original auto-interp paper's revision protocol, and that neurons which look like they
   fire on X very often only fire on a narrow subset of X that is hard to characterize compactly;
   forcing the neuron to fire on all of X would degrade the model.
2. *Activation magnitudes may carry the explanation.* If you cannot predict which activations
   should be strong versus weak, you may be missing a large part of what the neuron encodes.
   Gao's best guess from the results he has seen is that ablating *small* neuron activations
   substantially degrades model performance — i.e. weak activations cannot be ignored. He flags
   that this differs from Cammarata's ablation results and that tracking down the divergence
   seems worth doing (promising to share more concrete results soon).

**Simulation scores.** To claim understanding you must be able to *simulate* the neuron without
degrading the model: write a rubric ("dog literally on a bicycle = 4.5; dog and bicycle but no
riding = 3.2"), substitute simulated values for real activations, and compare. Cammarata argues
simulation scores are too demanding; Gao replies that this is largely an artifact of the
correlation-based simulation scoring in the original auto-interp paper, fixed almost entirely by
(a) measuring downstream KL instead of correlation and (b) scoring over a large dataset. KL also
avoids having to bin activations carefully, and it will not punish details that do not matter to
behavior — while still demanding an explanation for relations that do. He notes downstream KL
is preferable to loss delta, since offsetting bad modeling of some neurons with good modeling of
others can spuriously improve loss. He claims this scoring would have flagged known bad
polysemantic explanations as bad from the start — e.g. the car-and-dog reading of the
V-shaped-object neuron, which fails to fire on many V shapes and falsely fires on round dogs and
cars.

**Beyond neurons.** Explaining individual neurons is only part of the goal; the real target is
the computation between neurons. Circuit-finding still produces large, complicated, redundant
circuits that fail to explain most of the variance in model behavior. Gao's current best
explanation is superposition: concepts are stored in superposition and MLPs perform more than one
computation per neuron. He closes on the field's recurring failure mode — a lack of rigor, and a
tendency to over-index on qualitative results that look cool while overselling actual
understanding.

Noted examples in the piece: a neuron that activates on Colorado and also on Tornado (the Python
library, not tornadoes) — cited as hard to explain monosemantically as "Colorado + Tornado
library," because the Tornado activation is small relative to the Colorado/Rockies activations;
a first-layer neuron (L0/N4730) that is "clearly handling '-ado' words"; and a footnote on a
"COLORFUL" GPU-brand neuron that covers the CSS `color` property plus the GPU brand but not other
CSS color properties or other GPU brands.
