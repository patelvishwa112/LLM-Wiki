Root-cause analysis explains why a problem exists but not the potential consequences of a team's actions. A team might address a likely cause, only to create a new limitation or negatively impact another area. Without visibility into downstream effects and underlying assumptions, other product managers cannot assess these risks.
Before committing to a course of action based on root-cause analysis, product managers should map the causal chain, including past causes, future effects, and the assumptions linking them. This cause-and-effect map allows the team to review the logic and potential impacts before acting.
Work backward from the question
Write the open product question at the top. Ask for a likely cause. Ask why again for each answer. Keep going for at least five levels. Stop when the next why does not give you a deeper cause.
Add a branch when more than one cause seems likely. If PMs draw one chain, they may favor a neat story. When they add branches, they keep other causes open for review.
Treat each endpoint as a likely root cause. Add evidence to every link. Mark links with missing sources, challenged claims, or unknown ties. Another PM can then see where evidence ends and judgment begins.
Trace the action forward
Place the proposed action beside the chain. Write its first effect. Then trace the next effect. Continue through five orders of effects.
Add helpful and harmful branches. One action may ease the first problem but create a limit elsewhere. It may also weaken the result the team expects.
Each branch is a claim that needs judgment and support. The forward pass gives the team effects to inspect. It does not give them a forecast.
The full map has this shape:
plaintext
Open question
→ backward why-chain
→ root-cause candidates
→ evidence and gaps on each uncertain link
Proposed action
→ first-order consequences
→ second-order consequences
→ third-, fourth-, and fifth-order consequences
→ positive and negative branches
The two passes answer different questions. The backward pass asks why the problem may exist. The forward pass asks which effects the action may cause. In the last check, reviewers test the assumptions that join them.
Test every link
For each link, write the belief behind it. Add a second cause that may also fit. Then note the facts that would change your view. Other people can use these notes to inspect the case on the map.
Check whether cause and effect may run in reverse. Look for a factor behind both. Remove false detail. Do not treat a likely sequence as proof. Several causes may work together, so a single chain may hide part of the problem.
Another PM can point to one link and ask for its support. They can ask which other causes the PM checked and which links need a test. Reviewers now look at the chain, not how sure the PM sounds.
Show where you are guessing
Tracing five orders of effects can make weak guesses look well tested.
Mark each branch made by AI as a guess. Mark links that lack proof and keep other causes on the page. Use the map to choose which links need tests. The map can show where you lack proof, but it cannot prove the cause or predict the future.
AI can take each why-chain further, add helpful and harmful branches, and list beliefs for review. This helps when a PM's first answer leaves out more than one likely cause.
The PM still adds product context and judges each claim and source. With AI, the PM can review more paths but still owns every claim.
For the next choice, write the open question and work backward. Place the proposed action beside it and trace its effects forward. Mark each link that lacks support.
One PM may keep these steps in mind for one choice. A team that uses agents across many choices needs a review that PMs can repeat, shared standards, and each PM's product context.
