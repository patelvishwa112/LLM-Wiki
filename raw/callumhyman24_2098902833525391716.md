# How To Articulate Thoughts Like The Top 0.01%

**Author:** Callum · 2026-09-12 · https://x.com/callumhyman24/status/2098902833525391716

## Post

"As long as you're smart, you'll be successful!" 

Right?

Then James comes along, steals your idea, simply says it better, and wins the $5,500 prize from your debate competition.

4 years later, I'm still salty about this. He mogged me.

But James wasn't even special.

All he did was articulate his thoughts more intelligently than me. 

I've since consulted 50+ different companies on marketing and growth (several worth $10B+), all at the age of 19 years old.

Articulation is one of the main skills I've learned that allows my speech to be respected and heard, even by billionaire CEOs.

By the time you finish this article:

-> You'll understand how to also turn your complex thoughts into structured ideas that ANYONE can understand.

-> Learn the 3 communication frameworks that improved my articulation by 250%

-> How to improve your delivery (making communication 99% easier and more graceful)
Step 1: Complex thoughts -> simplified ideas

You see, a thought in your head can arrive as a whole myriad of things: 

A memory.

An opinion.

An example.

A conclusion you haven't even put into thoughts yet. 

This is because human brains are complicated...

Most of the time, it's nearly impossible to tell someone what exactly you're thinking. 

This is where the work begins. You have to decide what comes first, what matters, and what can wait. 

This is called structure.
Your explanation to someone might start on page 50 because you’ve already read the first 49 pages.
The person listening just opened the book... Ofc they're gonna be clueless.
Before an important conversation, open your notebook and write the answers to three questions.
What exactly am I saying?
Write the idea without trying to make it sound impressive.
“We should change the opening of the video because viewers can’t tell what the product does.”
That is something another person can understand and discuss.
“We need to optimize the narrative architecture to improve audience resonance” gives them several extra things to decode. 

This increases their mental load, which leads to their brain shutting off when you talk.

Specific words do more work.
What does this person need from me?
Who is your audience?

The same idea needs a different explanation depending on who is listening.
A founder may need to know whether the opening communicates the benefit.
An editor needs to know which shots to change.
A producer needs to know whether the change affects the schedule.
If you give everyone the same explanation, some of them will leave without the answer they needed.
Finish this sentence before you speak:
“After this conversation, I want them to understand, decide, or do ______.”
What is necessary to get them there?
Write down everything you could say. Then identify the main point, the strongest support, and the action you need.
Keep the rest available for questions.
Try explaining the idea in 60 seconds. Then try 30. Then write a single sentence that preserves the core meaning.
The sentence won’t contain every nuance. It should give the listener somewhere to start.
If you keep getting stuck on the same part, investigate it. You may need a better example, analogy, metaphor, more knowledge, or a clearer distinction.

Imagine you're in a sales call, and a prospect says, 

"I don't have the money."

"I don't have the time." 

These are objections that a good salesman would handle before they could even say them.

It's the same thing with articulation. 

Depending on who you're talking to:

decipher what questions they may have, and preemptively answer them in your speech. 
Step 2: Give your thoughts a structure
Under pressure, inventing both your answer and its structure is a lot to manage.
A framework gives you an order to follow.
That leaves more attention for the substance of what you’re saying, and the person listening.
Here are three you can use in different situations.
1. CCC: Context → Core idea → Connection
Use this when someone needs background before your idea will make sense.
Context: What do they need to know before you make your point?
Core idea: What is the main takeaway you want them to understand?
Connection: Why does that takeaway matter to them or the outcome you’re working toward?
For example:
“Our opening lists five features before showing what the product does.”
That’s the context.
“I think we should show the result first, then explain the features that make it possible.”
That’s the core idea.
“That would make the benefit easier for potential customers to understand.”
That’s the connection.
You’ve given them enough background to follow your recommendation and a reason to care about it.
Keep the context short. You don’t need to explain the entire history of the project before getting to the point.
2. PARA: Point → Action → Result → Ask
Use this when you want to support an idea with experience and help someone apply it.
Point: What is your recommendation or lesson?
Action: What did you, or someone else, do that illustrates it?
Result: What happened as a result of that action?
Ask: What question connects the lesson to the person listening?
Here’s a hypothetical example:
“We should agree on one main message before revising the script.”
That’s the point.
“On another project, the team wrote down the single takeaway they wanted viewers to remember before making more edits.”
That’s the action.
“It gave them a shared criterion for deciding which changes belonged.”
That’s the result.
“What’s the one thing we want someone to remember after watching our video?”
That’s the ask.
This gives your advice something concrete to stand on.
You’ve explained the idea, shown how it can work, and invited the other person to apply it.
Use a real example when you have one. If you’re describing what you expect to happen, make that clear.
3. POP: Pause → One takeaway → Propose a question
Use this when you’re put on the spot or don’t fully understand what someone means.
Pause: Take a moment to process what they said.
One takeaway: Share the part you understand, or your initial perspective.
Propose a question: Ask for the clarification you need.
For example, a client asks for more technical detail.
You pause.
Then:
“My takeaway is that you want the video to feel credible to a technical audience.”
Then:
“Which distinction are you worried we’d lose if we simplified it?”
Now you have something useful to discuss.
The client might be worried about credibility. They might also be correcting an actual error.
Your question helps you find out.
You don’t need to pretend you understand everything immediately.
Curiosity and clarification can strengthen the conversation.
You can also use POP when you disagree:
“We’ve changed the hook three times, and each revision adds another feature. I’m wondering whether we haven’t agreed on the main benefit yet. Which outcome matters most to the customer?”
You’re giving the other person room to explain what’s driving their feedback.
Expert articulation includes helping someone else clarify what they mean.
Which framework should you use?
CCC: “I need to explain this idea.”
PARA: “I need to show why this idea is useful.”
POP: “I need to understand more before answering.”

Practice choosing the structure that fits the moment.

Step 3: Make your delivery easier to follow
You can organize a strong answer and still bury it under speed, repetition, and unnecessary words.
Delivery gives the listener enough space to follow the structure you prepared.
Pause before the first sentence
You don’t have to begin the instant someone finishes asking a question.
Take a breath.
Identify your first point.
If you need longer, say:
“Let me think about that for a moment.”
For a complex question, you can also say:
“I have an initial view, but I’d want to check one thing before recommending it.”
That tells people where your thinking stands.
When you start speaking before you know your point, you can spend the next minute trying to find it aloud.
Sometimes that exploration is useful. In a decision meeting, tell people when you’re exploring rather than presenting a settled conclusion.
Let a finished point stay finished
You make a recommendation.
Nobody responds immediately.
So you explain it again.
Add another reason.
Soften it.
Add a qualification.
Now the recommendation sounds less settled than it did ten seconds ago.
Give people time to process.
If you need a response, ask a specific question:
“What concern would stop us from moving ahead?”
That gives the silence a purpose and the other person a useful way to respond.
Choose words that identify the thing
A bigger vocabulary is useful when it helps you become more precise.
“The design feels bad” could mean:
→ Cluttered.
→ Inconsistent.
→ Unreadable.
→ Wrong for the audience.
Those are different problems with different solutions.
Find the word that identifies the problem.
You don’t need to replace “I’m out of ideas” with a phrase that makes your friends wonder what happened to you.
Use language your audience understands. Explain unfamiliar terms when they’re necessary.
The test is simple:
Did the word make the meaning clearer?
Vary your pace and emphasis
Constant speed makes it harder to notice what matters.
Slow down for the recommendation.
Pause between separate points.
Let your voice carry some enthusiasm when you care about the idea.
Read this aloud:
“We can ship tomorrow. But the payment flow still needs testing.”
The pause gives the second sentence room to register.
You don’t need to perform every word.
Choose where the emphasis belongs.
Stay open when someone challenges you
A well-delivered correction is still a correction.
If someone points out a problem with your reasoning, clarify it.
If they’re right, update the recommendation.
“Good catch. That changes the timeline. Let’s revise it.”
People need to understand how you reached your position and what would change it.
Sounding certain about something you haven’t checked makes the conversation harder later.
Step 4: Practice the moments you actually struggle with
Watching articulate people gives you examples.
Speaking gives you practice.
For the next week, spend ten minutes a day on one explanation.
Pick something real:
A project update.
An opinion.
A request.
An answer you stumbled through recently.
Then:
1. Write the main point in one sentence.
2. Choose one of the three frameworks.
3. Record a one-minute explanation.
4. Listen back.
Ask yourself:
“Where did I actually make my point?”
“Which sentence repeated something I’d already said?”
“Where would someone unfamiliar with the topic get lost?”
5. Fix one issue and record it again.
You can also practice a phrase you wish came more naturally:
A clear recommendation.
A calm disagreement.
A specific question.
Use different examples so you learn the structure rather than memorize a speech.
When you admire another speaker, pay attention to the decisions underneath their delivery.
Where did they start?
What did they leave out?
Which example made the idea click?
Then explain something from your own life using that approach.
Over time, you’ll have more ideas you’ve already examined, examples you can explain, and ways to begin without searching for an opening.
That gives you more attention to spend on the person in front of you.
I still would have preferred the $5,500.

But James left me with a question worth taking into every important conversation:
“Have I made this easy for someone else to understand?”
Combine this with a great idea and you will be successful.
