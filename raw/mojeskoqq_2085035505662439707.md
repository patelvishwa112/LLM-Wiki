Picture a supermarket with one register open. Everyone waits in the same line, including the person holding a single carton of milk.
Your AI assistant works the same way. Step one, step two, step three, each waiting on the last. You watch it read carefully through a file the next step never needed. Then its memory fills up and it forgets where it started.
Half those steps never had to wait. They lined up because that is how you typed them.
The model is not weak. The shape is wrong: you built a line where you could have opened every register at once.
Graph engineering is the practice of choosing that shape on purpose. Before you hand a multi-step job to AI, you decide two things: what has to happen in order, and what can happen at the same time. That is the whole idea. Everything else here is detail.
As of May 28, 2026, Claude Code can build that shape for you. You describe the job in ordinary words, and Claude Code splits the work across dozens of small helpers, runs them at the same time, and brings back one finished answer. The official name is dynamic workflows. From here on I will call it a work plan.
The uncomfortable part first, so there is no surprise later. This buys width, not brains. Sixteen helpers will find more than one will; they will not think better. And it costs more: Anthropic warns plainly that a single run can burn meaningfully more of your usage than the same task in a normal conversation. The plan is not what gets expensive. A plan you never needed is.
What you will be able to do by the end
You will be able to explain what a graph is to someone else in a minute. You will know when it beats a loop and when it does not. You will be able to run work that goes wide instead of standing in line, using five prompts you can copy. And you will know where this breaks, so you do not learn it from your bill.
Bookmark this one. The prompts in Part 6 are long; you will not reproduce them from memory.
The road map:


Part 1. What a graph actually is
The point of this part: the entire theory, in two words and one test.
First, clear one thing up. "Graph" here has nothing to do with charts, bars, or lines on a screen. It is the other meaning of the word: a set of boxes connected by arrows. Think of a subway map, not a sales chart.
A node is one job for one helper. Check one file. Research one competitor. Write one section. One input, one output.
An edge is a dependency: "this job waits on that one." It is real only when the result is genuinely needed.
That is it. Nodes and edges. A graph is your work drawn as boxes and arrows, where boxes with no arrow between them can run at the same time.
Here is the shape you will actually be building, drawn out:
Three things to notice in that picture. The work splits into pieces that never talk to each other. Every piece is checked by someone who did not do it. And at the bottom there is one more job whose entire purpose is to gather everything into a single answer, which is why you get one report instead of five.
Now the test that makes this useful. Take any multi-step task of yours and, at every "and then," ask:
Does the next step actually need the previous one's result?
"Summarize this file and then check the weather" sounds like a sequence. The weather does not need the summary at all. Those two jobs were never required to wait on each other. The arrow you drew between them is fake, and the waiting is pure waste.
The physical version of the test: cover the previous step with your thumb. If the next one can still start, there is no edge between them.
Draw your current process and you will find two or three fake arrows in it. Almost everyone does, because we write steps in the order we thought of them, not in the order they actually depend on each other.
If every job on your list reads the result of the one before it, there is no graph here. The steps genuinely depend on each other, and that is fine. Everything below is for the opposite case.


Part 2. Loop versus graph: the real difference
The point of this part: understand what changes when you go from one to the other, and what it buys you.
A loop is one agent going around in a circle: do the work, check the result, fix what is wrong, go again. It looks like this:
That is the pattern most people learned first, and it is genuinely good. It turned AI from "types an answer and stops" into "keeps working until the job is done."
A graph is what you reach for when one loop is not enough. Instead of one agent going around many times, you have many jobs, some running at the same time, and a separate checker looking at the results. That is the picture from Part 1.
Put plainly: a loop makes one worker persistent. A graph puts several workers side by side and adds someone to check them.
Four things a graph gives you that a loop cannot.
Time. This is where the headline comes from, and it is just arithmetic. If checking one file takes two minutes, then twelve files in a line take about twenty-four minutes. Twelve files side by side take about two, because they finish together. The saving is not magic; it is what happens when nothing waits for its turn.
A clean memory per helper. Think of memory as a desk. A single agent working through forty files piles all forty on one desk, and by file thirty-nine it is working on top of a mountain of paper it can barely see past. Twelve helpers get twelve empty desks. The quality of the last file matches the quality of the first, and that alone often matters more than the speed.
Real checking. A loop checks itself, and self-checking is measurably weak; the numbers are in Part 8. A graph hands the result to someone who did not do the work and does not know the reasoning behind it. That is the single biggest quality gain here, and it is why every prompt in Part 6 has a separate checker in it.
Size. Some jobs simply do not fit on one desk: a whole codebase (all the code in a project), a hundred-file conversion, a question that needs six different searches. A graph splits them into pieces that do fit.
And two things a loop still wins at.
Cost. A loop is one agent. A graph is a dozen or more. If the job fits in a loop, use a loop.
Steering. In a loop you watch, interrupt, and redirect. In a graph you cannot: there is no user input in the middle of a run. You can stop or pause a run yourself, but you cannot answer a question halfway through, because it will not ask you one. You approve the shape at the start and read the result at the end.
The honest summary is one line. A graph does not make AI smarter. It makes it wider, and it lets you check it properly. Choose it when the work is genuinely wide, not because it sounds more advanced.


Part 3. Who this is for, and what you need to start
The point of this part: find yourself in one of these, and find out what it takes to actually use it.
A developer working alone or on a small team. You have a project nobody has fully read in a year. One helper per file finds things a single pass never will, because nothing gets tired and nothing gets skipped near the end.
Anyone who reviews other people's changes. Four reviewers with four different questions catch things one reviewer with a general opinion does not. Especially the question "what else in the project uses this and quietly behaves differently now."
Anyone facing a big mechanical job. Renaming something across a project, moving a hundred files to a new approach, ripping out an old call that nobody should use anymore. This is the most boring work in software and the one graphs are best at.
Anyone making a decision with real money behind it. No code required. Split the question into five angles, research them at the same time, then have every claim checked against its own source by someone who did not find it.
Anyone who publishes. Fact-checking a piece before it goes out is a perfect fan-out, meaning one job split into many that run at once: one checker per claim, each opening the actual source, plus a separate list of what could not be confirmed.
Anyone who inherited something they did not build. An old service, an acquired project, a departed colleague's work. Reading it takes weeks. Splitting the reading across a dozen helpers and merging their notes takes an afternoon.
Six things people actually run:
Search the whole project for one specific problem, with every finding attacked by three skeptics before it reaches the report.
Review your changes before you combine them with everyone else's work, through four different lenses at once, with a judge over them.
Convert a hundred files to a new approach, each in its own isolated copy, with tests as the checkpoint that decides whether each file is kept.
Hunt for bugs when you do not know how many there are, in rounds, until two rounds turn up nothing new.
Research a decision, cross-checking every claim against its source and listing what could not be verified.
Read an unfamiliar project: one helper per folder, each producing a short map, merged into one document.
The first five are in Part 6, ready to copy.
What you need before any of this works
One honest condition, because it decides whether the rest of this article is for you. All of this runs inside Claude Code, which is a developer tool you use from a terminal, the black window where you type commands. There is no app with buttons for it yet.
So if you write code, you are already set up. If you do not, the research prompt in Part 6 has nothing to do with code and is genuinely useful on its own, but you will still have to install Claude Code from Anthropic's site and type commands into a terminal to run it. That is the entry fee. It is a small one, and plenty of non-developers pay it, but you should know about it before Part 4 rather than after.
And who this is not for, briefly: anyone with a small task, anyone who wants to approve each step, and anyone still figuring out what the problem even is. Part 10 covers why.


Part 4. What to switch on
The point of this part: three conditions, a minute to check them.
Claude Code version 2.1.160 or newer. Type claude --version in your terminal to see it. The current release is 2.1.220, so just update and stop thinking about numbers.
A paid plan. This does not exist on the free tier.
The toggle. On Max, Team, and Enterprise it is already on. On Pro you switch it on yourself: type /config and flip the Dynamic workflows row.
Now the detail that makes half the guides online wrong. This used to start when you wrote the word "workflow" in your request. That word is not the command anymore. The command is ultracode, and it works from version 2.1.160 onward, which is why the list above says 160 rather than 154, even though the feature itself landed slightly earlier. If you simply ask in plain language, something like "run this as one work plan," Claude will understand you in any version.
One caveat if you plan to wire this into automation. The keyword only fires in a prompt you typed by hand. It will not fire from a scheduled task, an external call, or a comment on someone's proposed change.
One more setting worth knowing up front. In /config there is a Dynamic workflow size row: a hint about how many helpers to aim for. Since version 2.1.219 the default is medium, meaning "fewer than fifteen." It is a suggestion, not a limit: if your prompt explicitly asks for one helper per file across twenty files, Claude Code follows your number. But the pressure from the setting is real, so before the bigger prompts in Part 6, set /config workflowSizeGuideline=large. Otherwise the plan may skimp and hand one helper four files at once.


Part 5. Your first run
The point of this part: see the whole thing with your own eyes in five minutes, without an unexpected bill.
Open a real project you know, so the result means something. Do it on a fresh branch, which is a separate copy of the project you can throw away without losing anything. The helpers edit files on their own without asking; more on that below.
Paste this, with your own path:
ultracode: audit every route file under src/routes/ for missing auth
checks. spawn one agent per file, then run an independent verifier on
each finding before it reaches the report. analyze a maximum of 12
files to start.
In plain words: check every file in this folder for a missing permission check, put one helper on each file, and before any finding reaches the report, hand it to a different helper for an independent look. Start with no more than twelve files.
That last line works as a fuse. On the first run you want to learn how this behaves and what it costs, not get a bill for the entire project. Twelve is not arbitrary either: it fits comfortably inside the default size setting, so the plan will not start cutting corners on helpers.
Claude does not just go. It shows you the plan in stages first: how many helpers, what runs after what. Read that plan. It is the only moment where you see the shape of the work before paying for it. Then hit "Yes, run it."
The helpers go off to work in the background, each with its own separate memory, so they do not crowd each other or trip over someone else's work. Up to sixteen run at once (fewer on an older or slower computer), and the rest wait their turn. Meanwhile you keep working in the same window.
A few minutes later you get one answer, not twelve separate reports. Everything in between stayed with the helpers; only the final answer came back to you.
The screen, and the key that pays for everything
Type /workflows at any point. You get the list of runs and can open any of them. Inside, every stage shows how many helpers are working, how much has been spent, and how long it has been running.
Four keys matter: p pauses and resumes, x stops, r restarts a helper that stalled, and s saves the run.
s deserves its own paragraph, because it is half the value here. A good run gets saved and from then on it runs as an ordinary command, /name. Keep it in the project folder, where everyone who downloads the project gets it, or in your personal settings, where it stays yours. The second time, the same review costs you one line instead of ten minutes of typing. A good prompt from Part 6 plus the s key gives you a personal tool nobody else has.
Three things worth knowing now rather than later.
First: while the Claude Code window stays open, pausing and resuming is cheap, and some finished work will not be redone. But if you quit Claude Code mid-run, it starts over from scratch. An unfinished run is not kept between program restarts. Do not launch a big job five minutes before you close the laptop.
Second, and this is the important one. Helpers inside a work plan get their file edits auto-approved, whatever your usual settings say. You cannot take that right away from them; it is how the platform is built. A "change nothing" line inside a prompt is a request, not a guard. Your one real guard is a separate branch or copy of the project where every change is visible.
Third: the helpers run on the same model as the session you launched them from. Since version 2.1.219 the default top-tier model is Claude Opus 5, which is more capable and costs more per unit of work than the mid-tier models. So check what you are running on before you call fifteen helpers.


Part 6. Five ready-made prompts
The point of this part: the part you came for. Copy it, replace what is in square brackets, run it.
Under each prompt there is a step-by-step breakdown of what it does and why it is built that way, so you know what you are paying for.
They all share one shape: spread the work wide, then have the result checked by someone who did not do it. What changes is what you are hunting.
Before prompts 6.1, 6.3, 6.4, and 6.5, set /config workflowSizeGuideline=large. They are big, and on the default setting the plan starts cutting corners. Only 6.2 is light enough to fit the default.
6.1 Full sweep with skeptics
Use it when: you suspect the same problem is scattered across the whole project and you want a list without invented entries.
ultracode: run a security sweep over src/ in three phases.

phase 1, map: list the files that handle user input, authentication, or
database access. cap the list at 40 files, ranked by how much untrusted
data they touch. report the rule you used to pick them, how many files
matched but did not make the cap, and which categories you excluded
(tests, generated, vendored) with a count for each. do not list every
excluded file.

phase 2, hunt: spawn exactly one agent per file, all in parallel, and
do not batch several files into one agent. each returns findings as
structured items with file, line, severity, and a one-line reason, one
item per line. no prose, no summaries, no "consider reviewing this".

phase 3, kill: rank all findings by severity and take the top 40, then
run three independent skeptics on each. every skeptic returns one
verdict word plus one line of evidence, nothing else.

skeptic A, is this code path reachable from outside the system?
  UNREACHABLE (say what blocks it) / REACHABLE (name the entry point) /
  UNKNOWN.
skeptic B, does the framework or a library already handle this case?
  HANDLED (name the exact mechanism) / NOT_HANDLED / UNKNOWN.
skeptic C, write the exact input that triggers it.
  TRIGGERED (give the literal input) / NO_INPUT_FOUND / UNKNOWN.

apply these rules in order, do not vote:
- A says UNREACHABLE or B says HANDLED, the finding is dead. one clear
  disproof kills it, the other two skeptics do not outweigh that.
- otherwise C says TRIGGERED, the finding is confirmed.
- otherwise the finding is unverified: neither confirmed nor dead.
- UNKNOWN never confirms and never kills anything.

report confirmed findings grouped by severity, each with file, line, and
the literal input from skeptic C. then three separate sections: dead
findings with the disproof that killed them, unverified findings with
what was missing, and findings below the top 40 cut that were never
checked.
How it works:
First a map: which files take in outside data at all. No more than forty, with an honest account of what was left out.
Then one helper per file, plus an explicit ban on lumping files together. Without that line the plan quietly cuts corners and you never see it happen.
Then three attackers per finding, each with a different job. One figures out whether an outsider can reach that code at all. One checks whether the framework already covers it. One tries to write the literal input that triggers it.
And the important part: rules instead of voting.
The rules deserve their own paragraph, because nearly every guide gets this wrong. The usual advice is "keep the finding if two out of three skeptics fail to refute it." It sounds rigorous, but it does not hold up: the skeptics are answering different questions. If the first one proves nothing outside can reach that code, the finding is dead no matter what the other two say. One clear disproof beats two shrugs. So the prompt uses an order of precedence, not a vote: first see who killed it, then who confirmed it, and "unknown" counts for neither side.
Second thing worth noticing: a finding is only confirmed once the third skeptic produces the literal input that breaks it. If nobody can show you what breaks it, there is probably nothing to break. That single rule kills half the pretty-but-imaginary findings.
Third: the cap of forty findings to verify. Without it the arithmetic eats the run. Forty files at five findings each is two hundred findings, times three skeptics, six hundred helpers. The hard ceiling on one run is a thousand helpers.
6.2 Review through four different lenses
Use it when: you are about to combine your work with everyone else's and you want a ranked list of what should actually stop you, not a general opinion.
ultracode: review my changes against the base branch. resolve the base
as `git merge-base HEAD origin/main` (use origin/master or origin/develop
if main does not exist) and review the three-dot diff from there to HEAD.
state the base commit you resolved before reporting anything.

run four reviewers in parallel, each with one lens, none of them seeing
the others' output:
- correctness: does it do what the change description claims.
- security: what a hostile input does here.
- performance: at what input size or request rate this stops working.
- blast radius: for each exported symbol whose signature or behavior
  this diff changes, grep the repo for its call sites and report only
  the ones that now get different behavior without a compile error.

each finding needs file, line, severity, and a concrete failure scenario:
for logic and input, the literal input; for a race, the two operations
and the order that breaks it; for load, the size or rate at which it
falls over. a finding with none of those gets dropped before it reaches
me.

if the diff exceeds 2000 changed lines, split each lens across files
instead of giving one agent the whole diff.

then run one judge over all four sets. the judge removes duplicates,
resolves disagreements by re-reading the code itself instead of trusting
either reviewer, and ranks what is actually worth blocking the merge.

do not change any code and do not run any git command that modifies
state. finish with a short section on what you deliberately did not
review and why.

How it works:
The first paragraph solves a boring but expensive problem: which point to measure your changes from. Leave it unsaid and four reviewers can pick different starting points, after which the judge spends your money reconciling disagreements that do not exist.
Then four reviewers look from different sides, none seeing the others' conclusions.
That fourth lens, "who else uses this code," catches the most expensive class of bug: the code works, the tests pass, and behavior quietly changed for someone at the other end of the project.
Every finding has to arrive with something concrete or it gets thrown out.
Then the judge, who settles disputes by re-reading the code rather than by vote.
The "change nothing" line is useful here, but remember Part 5: it is a request, not a guard. Which is exactly why you still run this on a separate branch, where the list of changed lines will show you if a reviewer touched anything.
Of the five, this is the lightest: five helpers, nothing gets written, and it fits inside the default setting.
6.3 The same edit in a hundred places
Use it when: renaming something across a project, moving everything to a new approach, stripping an outdated piece out of a hundred files.
This is the most dangerous of the five. It is exactly where people who copy pretty examples off the internet get burned, which is why it carries the most guardrails.
ultracode: migrate every file under src/components/ from [old approach]
to [new approach].

setup: create at most 4 isolated worktrees, not one per file, and split
the file list across them. in each worktree install dependencies and
confirm that `[your test command]` passes on one untouched file BEFORE
migrating anything. if that baseline check fails in a worktree, stop and
tell me instead of migrating: the environment is broken, not the code.

the only whole-repo git commands you may run are `git worktree add` and
`git worktree remove`. never run git stash, git reset, git checkout of a
branch, git clean, or git restore on anything wider than the single file
you are reverting.

for each file: make the change, then run `[your test command]` on the
test file that matches this source file by the project's convention
(for example src/components/Foo.tsx -> Foo.test.tsx or
__tests__/Foo.test.tsx). never run the full suite for a single file. if
no matching test file exists, do not migrate that file: put it in a
list called "no test, not migrated" and move on.

tests pass, commit that one file in its worktree. tests fail, read the
failure, fix it once, run again. still failing, revert that file
completely and mark it unresolved. never mark a file done on a failing
test. never disable, skip, or rewrite a test, never update snapshots,
never change an expected value, and never add .skip, .only, xit, or
@pytest.mark.skip.

when all worktrees are done, apply the passing commits back onto my
current branch in the main working tree as one patch, leave unresolved
files untouched, and print the exact command you used.

then run one agent per 10 migrated files. each reads the git diff of its
files, old against new, and flags any place where the new code does
something different rather than the same thing written differently:
changed defaults, changed order of operations, a dropped edge case, a
different value on the error path. one final agent merges their lists.

finish with four lists: migrated, fixed on the second attempt,
unresolved with the actual error, and "no test, not migrated".
How it works, and why it is this long. One term first: a worktree is simply a separate copy of the project that lives on your disk at the same time as the original, so a helper can work in one without touching the other.
Four copies of the project, not one per file. A hundred copies means installing everything a hundred times and filling your disk. One copy per group, not one per helper, is what the largest project ever built this way settled on. That story is in Part 9.
An environment check before any work starts. Without it you get this: a fresh copy has none of the supporting libraries installed, tests fail on everything, and the plan dutifully marks all hundred files unresolved. You paid for a hundred helpers and got nothing.
Two commands allowed, everything else banned. Otherwise you get the absurd version: you ban whole-repo git commands, and the ban takes out git worktree add, the very command that creates the isolated copies.
An exact rule for which tests to run. Leave it out and the helper either runs every test in the project once per file, or decides there are no tests and skips the check entirely. Both are bad; the second is worse.
A separate "no test, not migrated" list. Without it, files with no tests quietly land in "done" with nothing checking them at all, which is the exact hole the whole prompt exists to close.
Getting the work back. This is the most commonly forgotten piece: the work happened in separate copies, and your actual working folder still has none of it.
A long ban on massaging tests, including snapshots, which are saved copies of the expected result. Refreshing those is the easiest way to make a failing test pass without fixing anything, and it is the most common shortcut a helper takes.
The final pass reads the difference between old and new, not just the new file. If you read only the new text, you cannot tell whether behavior changed; there is nothing to compare against.
6.4 Hunting when you do not know how many there are
Use it when: you suspect a part of the project is full of problems but have no idea whether there are three of them or thirty. A normal request gives you exactly as many as you asked for and stops.
ultracode: find bugs in [module or folder]. i do not know how many are
there, so do not stop at a fixed number.

work in rounds, at most 5 rounds. in each round send four hunters at
once, with different angles: one reads the code for logic errors, one
reads the tests to find what is never covered, one traces what happens
on empty, huge, and malformed input, one looks for state that two things
can touch at the same time.

after each round, build a ledger of everything seen so far, confirmed
and rejected alike, as file:line plus one line of description. pass that
ledger into every hunter's prompt in the next round with this
instruction: do not report anything already in this list and do not
re-argue a rejected entry. also dedupe after the round as a backstop for
near-duplicates.

in every round after the first, split the module by subdirectory and
give each hunter a different slice, so no hunter re-reads the same files
two rounds in a row.

verify at most 15 findings per round with two independent skeptics that
each try to disprove the finding, and keep only what survives both.

stop when two rounds in a row produce nothing new, or after round 5. if
round 5 still produces new findings, stop anyway and tell me the list
was still growing when you stopped. finish with how many rounds it took,
how many findings each round produced, and what the last round was still
arguing about.
How it works:
Hunting in rounds rather than one pass, because you do not know the size of the problem in advance.
Four different approaches per round: logic, what tests never cover, behavior on empty and malformed input, and places two things can touch at the same time.
The ledger of what has already been seen goes into the next round as input. That is the key line. Removing duplicates after a round means you already paid for them. Telling the hunter up front means it never goes there.
And it has to include the rejected findings, not just the confirmed ones. Compare against only the confirmed ones and every rejected finding comes back each round, so you pay for it twice.
Different slices per round, otherwise round two is a word-for-word repeat of round one.
Hard ceilings: five rounds, fifteen verifications per round. "Stop when nothing new turns up" is not by itself a guarantee of stopping.
The honest cost of this design: a rejected finding stays rejected for the rest of the run. If a skeptic got it wrong, that mistake is locked in. Which is why the last line asks what the final round was still arguing about.
6.5 Researching a decision, no code involved
Use it when: real money rides on a decision, and normal search gives you nice articles with no sources.
Try the built-in command first: /deep-research your question. It already fans searches across several angles, cross-checks the sources it finds, votes on each claim, and returns a cited report with whatever did not survive filtered out. Do not pay for a hundred helpers to rebuild that.
The prompt below is for when the built-in is not enough on three specific counts: you want your angles rather than the ones picked for you, you want the result as a file you keep rather than a message in a chat, and you want it ranked by impact on the decision rather than by strength of evidence.
ultracode: i need decision-grade research on [your question].

phase 1: split the question into five angles that do not overlap and
name them before searching.

phase 2: one researcher per angle, all in parallel. each returns at most
6 claims, ranked by importance. every claim comes back with a source
link and a date. a claim with no source does not travel to the next
phase.

phase 3: take at most the top 20 claims across all angles and run two
checkers on each, with different jobs. checker A opens the source and
confirms the claim is actually in it and is not a paraphrase of
something weaker. checker B searches for a newer or contradicting
source. drop anything that fails either check. list the claims below the
cut as "not verified, below the cut".

write the result to ./research.md in the current working directory and
change no other file. four sections: what is solid, what is contested
and by whom, what changed recently, and "what i could not verify",
listing every claim that died and why. rank the solid findings by how
much they should move the decision, not by how interesting they are.
How it works:
Five non-overlapping angles, one researcher each, all at once.
A hard rule: a claim with no link and date does not move on.
Two checkers per claim with different jobs. One opens the source and confirms it really says that, rather than something weaker. One hunts for a newer or contradicting source.
Caps on the counts so the run does not balloon.
The result is a file with four sections, one of which is an explicit list of what could not be verified.
That "what I could not verify" section is worth half the value of the whole run. Normal search hands you confident prose that hides where the ground is missing. Here the missing ground is written out as a list.
One honest caveat. You will see those five angles in the finished report, not before the search starts, and you cannot influence them mid-run. The next part explains why.


Part 7. Seven lines that make any prompt stronger
The point of this part: short additions that turn an average result into a usable one. Bolt them onto your own prompts.
analyze a maximum of N files to start The fuse. Put it on any first run.
return findings as structured items with file, line, severity and a one-line reason, one item per line, no prose Forces a list instead of an essay. A list you can compare and count; an essay you have to reread.
a finding with no concrete failure scenario gets dropped Cuts the pretty guesses. But spell out what counts as concrete, or this line quietly removes your most expensive bugs. For logic and input it is a literal input. For two things touching the same data at once, it is the two operations and the order that breaks them. For load, it is the size or rate at which things fall over.
list separately what you could not verify Often worth more than the findings, because it shows you where not to lean.
never disable, skip or rewrite a test to make it pass, and never update snapshots or change an expected value Closes the most common shortcut a helper takes. The short version is not enough: refreshing the saved expected results is the way around it.
save the result to ./<file>.md and stop, change nothing else Your exit point. Remember Part 5: it is a request, not a guard, so run it on a branch where every change is visible.
Before a long run, put whatever the helpers will need on your allow list, the set of commands they may run without stopping to ask you. That usually means the test command, the tools that show changed lines, and web search. Otherwise the run stops at the first permission question and waits for you.
Now the thing even experienced people get wrong. You cannot ask for a plan that "stops halfway, shows me, and waits for my approval." The documentation says it outright: there is no user input mid-run. You can pause it yourself with p, but the plan cannot stop and ask you anything. The only thing that halts it without you is a tool permission question, and that is not a feature; it is how you accidentally freeze a run for an hour while you are away from the desk.
If you need a point where you decide, there is exactly one working approach: split the work into two runs. The first gathers and writes the result to a file. You read it. You launch the second yourself, based on what you read.
Write "show me and wait" and you get a plan that either never stops, or stops in the wrong place.


Part 8. What breaks here
The point of this part: four failures, three of which are waiting for you in week one.
A helper cannot see its own mistake
That is not a figure of speech; it has been measured.
In a study by Google DeepMind and the University of Illinois, models were asked to review their own answers with no outside hint. Results did not improve; they dropped. GPT-4 scored 95.5 percent on grade-school math problems, then 91.5 after one round of self-review, then 89.0 after two. It was "correcting" right answers into wrong ones.
A separate measurement shows what is going on: on average, two out of three times a model does not notice its own mistake. It fixes that exact same mistake reliably when someone else points it out.
This is the strongest single argument for a graph over a loop, and it is why Part 2 lists checking as the biggest quality gain. The checking has to come from a separate helper that did not watch the work being done, and it needs a different question. Not "is this good?" but "does this source exist?" and "does this happen again on a second try?" and "what happens with hostile input?" Hand the checker the whole history of the work and it has nothing left to check against.
That is exactly why in all five prompts above the checkers are separate helpers with different questions. It is not decoration; it is the reason the prompt is written that way.
Helpers getting in each other's way
Not theory. On the Bun project, covered in Part 9, it went wrong on the very first attempt: one helper hid the unsaved edits, another pulled them back out at the same moment, and a third rolled everything back to the old state. They were all working in one folder and erasing each other's work.
What saved it was not a cleverer prompt; it was structure. First, a whole class of commands was banned, anything that can hide or roll back someone else's work. Then the work was split across separate copies of the project: four copies, sixteen helpers in each.
Three questions to answer before you launch, if the helpers will write and not just read:
Where does each one physically work?
How do the results come back together?
What happens when two of them disagree?
If you have no answers, the plan will not survive growth. It will just fail faster. 
The bill
These runs draw on your normal plan, but they consume it differently. Anthropic states this directly: a single run can use meaningfully more than the same task in conversation.
There is no published "N times more expensive" figure from Anthropic, and the "15x" number going around comes from a report about an entirely different system. So trust your screen, not the posts: the spend per helper is visible in /workflows while the run is still going.
What actually keeps the bill down:
First run on a small slice. One folder instead of the whole project, a dozen files instead of all of them.
Ceilings written into the prompt itself, as in Part 6. Verify the top forty findings, not all of them. No more than five rounds. No more than six claims per researcher.
The Large workflow warning. Since version 2.1.203 it appears when a run schedules more than 25 helpers, or when the projected volume of text gets unusually large. It stops nothing; it just says: look at what you launched.
You stop reading
The quietest failure. With one helper you more or less skim what it did. With sixteen producing results four times faster than you read, the gap between "done" and "read" widens every hour.
That is not a hypothesis. It is exactly where the story in the next part ran aground.


Part 9. How the big story actually ended
The point of this part: see the real ceiling of this technology, in the case everyone wrote about and almost everyone got wrong.
Jarred Sumner rewrote Bun, a JavaScript engine, from one programming language into another using parallel Claude helpers. From his own account: 535,496 lines of code to start with, eleven days of work from May 3 to May 14, 2026.
There were about fifty work plans, one after another without a break. Never more than four ran at the same time, sixteen helpers each: roughly sixty-four at the peak. Over a million lines of code were added.
Cost: about $165,000 in tokens at Anthropic's API pricing: 5.9 billion
uncached input tokens, 690 million output, 72 billion cached reads.
That is the model bill, not anyone's salary. Note that this covers those eleven days only. An independent analysis by Tom Lockwood on July 27, 2026 shows the work after the merge cost considerably more, with his estimate closer to $800,000.
Now the three things that matter more than any number.
First: why it worked at all. Bun's tests are written so that they do not depend on the language the engine is written in. So the rewrite happened under a live safety net of tests rather than by guesswork. Without that, the story would have ended on day two. Which is the rule you can take home: any large automated overhaul needs one point you cannot argue with.
Second: who read the result. The line-by-line reading was done by other helpers, not people: each implementer had two or more reviewers whose whole job was to pick holes. Sumner says he spent most of those eleven days going through the outputs by hand looking for problems. Not launch-and-walk-away, but not human review either.
Third: how it ended. This is where the headlines and reality part ways. Inside Claude Code the new version has been running since June 17 and, in Sumner's own words, barely anyone noticed anything except a startup time roughly 10 percent faster on Linux.
But there is still no public release of Bun in the new language as of August 1, 2026. The last shipped version dates to May 12. The project's automated helper has a queue of about 2,500 finished but not yet merged pieces of work. Nineteen bugs surfaced after the merge and all were fixed, but the release is on hold until it is ready.
And the reaction. Andrew Kelley, who created the language it was rewritten from, called the result "a million lines of unreviewed slop" and doubted the tests were enough to catch bugs across that much code. He later edited his own conclusion and apologized for the personal tone, but the substance of the question stands. On the other side, Mitchell Hashimoto pointed out that no engineer at a comparable salary would have reached those milestones in eleven days. And Simon Willison put the whole story into one question: how do you review a change of a million lines?
That question is the real ceiling of this technology. Not sixteen helpers, not a thousand. The ceiling is how much you can actually read.


Part 10. When you do not need this
The point of this part: do not pay for a shape you never needed.
The task is small. One fix, one question. Here the plan just burns extra money; one helper is faster and cheaper.
You want control at every step. The entire point is that the work runs wide without you, and you cannot insert your approval into the middle, as covered in Part 7.
You do not understand the problem yet. When you are still feeling out the problem, you want one helper you lead by hand, not a dozen locking in a plan before you know what you are doing.
The steps really do depend on each other. If every step reads the previous result, there is nothing to split, and coordination still costs money. Check it with the same thumb from Part 1.
In all four cases, a loop is the better tool. That is not a downgrade; it is picking the right shape.


Cheat sheet
Take this and use it without rereading the article:
A node is one job, an edge is a dependency. Find jobs that never read each other's output. If there are none, use a loop.
Check three things: version 2.1.160 or newer, a paid plan, the Dynamic workflows toggle in /config on Pro.
Trigger it with ultracode or just ask in your own words. The word "workflow" is not the command anymore.
Run the first one on a small slice and on a separate branch: one folder, a dozen files.
Read the stage plan before you approve. It is the only free look at the shape of the work.
Checkers are separate helpers, each with its own question. You cannot take away their right to edit files; inside a plan, edits are always auto-approved. "Change nothing" is a request, not a guard. The guard is a separate branch.
Demand something concrete from every finding: what exactly breaks it. No answer, no finding.
If helpers write files, give them copies per group rather than per helper, and ban destructive commands.
Keep one point in the plan you cannot argue with: a test that passed, not a helper's opinion.
You cannot insert your approval mid-run. If you need a pause, split it into two runs through a file.
Write ceilings into the prompt itself, and watch the spend in /workflows during the run, not after.
Save a good run with s. From then on it costs one command.
Three questions before you start:
Where does my work split into pieces that never read each other?
What in here checks reality instead of checking another helper?
What happens if two helpers get into the same file?


The takeaway
Someone writing prompts asks a question. Someone doing graph engineering decides the shape of the answer.
The line was never the ceiling. It is just the first shape everyone reaches for, because it matches how we type: one line, one thing at a time. The loop was the first real step past that, and it was a good one. The graph is the next: not one worker who keeps going, but several who go at once and one who checks them.
Once you can see where your work diverges, the question changes. Not "how do I get the helper to do more," but "where can this be split, and what checks it afterward."
Width is one command away now. Attention is not. That stays with you, and the million-line question is about exactly that.
