# Pattern Recognition as Mastery: Michael Larson, Chunking, and Pattern Interrupts

**Source:** Tim Denning (@Tim_Denning), 2026-09-19. A long motivational X article that uses a 1984 game-show exploit and chess expertise research to argue pattern recognition is a learnable skill rather than a gift.

Denning's central definition is the useful part: pattern recognition is compressed experience, not intelligence. His proof case is the chess experiment — grandmasters rebuild a real mid-game position after a five-second look nearly perfectly, but score no better than novices on randomly scattered pieces. Nothing photographic is happening; the board simply resolves into five or six familiar chunks. Where a beginner sees 25 pieces, the master sees formations seen thousands of times. The corollary is what makes the piece actionable: if the mechanism is a library, the library can be built.

The narrative spine is Michael Larson, an unemployed ice cream truck driver who in 1984 taped CBS's Press Your Luck frame by frame for weeks and found the "random" board actually cycled through five repeating patterns, with two squares that never held a Whammy and awarded a bonus spin. He memorized the sequences, drilled his timing, and walked off with $110,237 — the largest one-day game-show haul at the time. CBS investigated for cheating, found no rule against paying attention, paid him, reprogrammed the board, and shelved the episode for roughly two decades. Denning reads it as feedback-loop economics: the tape let Larson check every guess against ground truth, which is the difference between twenty years of experience and one year repeated twenty times.

The prescriptive half is a six-part practice list — high-volume active reps, tight feedback, proximity to raw data instead of summaries, naming and filing patterns as you catch them, studying across domains so the same shape shows up in "new" problems, and reading history for rhymes. He adds pattern interrupts (from Robbins) as the tool for breaking bad loops, illustrated by grabbing and ringing a gold bell on stage to short-circuit a panic attack before a 2,000-person speech, and closes on the claim that the highest form is pattern recognition plus synthesis — connecting patterns, not just spotting them.

## Why it matters

The piece is a reminder of what "taste" in AI work actually is: a chunk library built from reviewed traces, labeled failures, and tight eval loops. Its practical transfer is the feedback requirement — teams that only accumulate raw experience without checking outcomes against a tape ("20 years of experience is one year lived 20 times") build no library at all. The cross-domain section also maps cleanly onto agent design, where the useful moves are usually borrowed shapes from operating systems, compilers, or control theory rather than novel inventions.

## Key points

- Michael Larson's 1984 Press Your Luck exploit: the board cycled five repeating patterns and two safe bonus-spin squares; payoff was $110,237, then the largest single-day game-show win, and no rule was broken.
- Chess chunking evidence: masters reconstruct real positions after five seconds but perform like novices on random positions — expertise is retrieval of familiar formations, not raw memory.
- Denning's definition to keep: pattern recognition is compressed experience, "a library," and libraries are built rather than inherited.
- Pattern interrupts break unconscious loops — his example is ringing a stage bell mid-panic to reset before a 2,000-person talk.
- Six build practices: extreme active reps, tight feedback loops, raw data over summaries, naming and collecting patterns, cross-field study, and history as a pattern source.
- His claim about ceiling: pattern recognition alone is insufficient; pattern recognition plus synthesis is what produces new problem-solving.
