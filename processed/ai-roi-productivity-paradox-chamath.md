# Why AI Is Booming but Productivity Isn't

**Source:** Chamath Palihapitiya (@chamath), 2026-09-25. A post accompanying Social Capital's "AI ROI" deep dive, arguing the productivity gap is a management problem rather than a model problem.

The argument starts from arithmetic that makes AI look absurdly cheap: Ramp's AI Index tracks AI spend across 70,000+ US businesses and finds the median company paying **$12.50 per employee per month** against roughly **$8,500** in monthly employee cost. That means AI pays for itself if it makes someone **0.15% more productive** — about **three extra productive minutes per week**. The token bill is a rounding error; the real costs are change management, retraining, and the rest of the corporate machinery, which is precisely why they don't appear as AI line items.

The value side is where the thesis bites. A P&G example has one person with AI producing product proposals as good as a two-person team without it, and MIT economists are cited for the finding that automation pays when AI steps are *grouped* so a human checks the work once rather than after every step. But if AI mainly compresses production time while a corporate week is consumed by coordination — meetings, alignment, sign-offs — then handing back three minutes changes nothing: the minutes flow into another meeting or a legal review queue and the return is zero. Hence the line that humans may be the bottleneck to AI ROI, and the hypothesis that AI *multiplies* output rather than adding to it, which would widen the gap between the best firms and everyone else.

The strongest evidence in the post is a 2026 experiment with **515 startups**, where every firm received identical AI tools, training, and support, and a random half additionally saw how peer firms reorganised their work around AI. For the typical startup revenue barely moved in either arm; almost all gains concentrated in the top decile of firms, and the treatment group pulled far enough ahead to reach **1.9× the control group's revenue**. Same tools, different work design.

He closes with two historical anchors. Bill Gates in 1995: automation applied to an efficient operation magnifies the efficiency, and applied to an inefficient one magnifies the inefficiency. And Solow's 1987 quip about seeing the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics — followed by the resolution: US productivity grew ~**1.5%/yr from 1973–1995**, then ~**3.3%/yr from 1995–2003** once work was reorganised around the machines. Musk's five-step method is offered as the operating discipline (question, delete, simplify, speed up, automate, in that order), with Tesla's mistake of automating processes that later proved unnecessary as the cautionary case.

## Why it matters

For anyone selling or buying AI, the constraint has moved from capability to organisational throughput: the marginal dollar is better spent on deleting and simplifying steps than on better models or more seats. For investors, it explains why aggregate productivity statistics stay flat while individual firms compound — and why the dispersion between adopters is a better signal than the mean. It also gives a defensible timeline: as in 1995–2003, the gains arrive when process redesign catches up, not when the tool ships.

## Key points

- Median AI spend: **$12.50/employee/month** (Ramp AI Index, 70,000+ US businesses) vs ~**$8,500/employee/month** in labour cost.
- Break-even productivity requirement: **~0.15%**, roughly **three productive minutes per week**.
- P&G: one AI-assisted person matched a two-person team on product proposals.
- MIT: automation pays when AI steps are batched so a human checks once, not per step.
- 2026 experiment, 515 startups, identical tools: typical firm's revenue barely moved; top 10% captured nearly all gains; treatment arm reached **1.9× control revenue**.
- Gates (1995) and Solow (1987) framing; productivity went **1.5%/yr (1973–1995)** → **3.3%/yr (1995–2003)** after reorganisation.
- Recommended order: question → delete → simplify → speed up → automate; Tesla automated unnecessary processes by reversing it.
