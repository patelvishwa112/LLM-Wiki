# Karpathy: Doing Well in Your Courses (the 85% Heuristic)

**Source:** Kian Kyars (@neuralkian), 2026-10-12. A one-line post pointing undergraduates at Andrej Karpathy's "Doing well in your courses" guide; the substance lives on the linked Stanford page.

The guide is tactical before it is philosophical. Sleep ~7.5 hours because consolidation happens overnight, so study starts days ahead (his budget is ~3 days for a midterm, ~6 for a final); write out every topic with a time estimate so the early material doesn't eat the schedule; read past exams from the same professor to infer question style; and treat reproduction as the test of understanding — close the book and rewrite the derivation, since reading it and being able to produce it use different memory. Study alone first and collaborate near the end, and don't only study with stronger students, because explaining to weaker ones is where the learning happens. Test-day advice is equally concrete: coffee and food two hours out, easy questions first, box the answer, be neat for the human marking it, never leave early.

The closing section is the part people quote. Nobody cares about your grades unless they're bad, and the smartest student is the one who gets 85% across every course — roughly a 4.0 without over- or under-studying — and redirects the saved time into things that compound: an internship, research with a professor who will then write a letter saying you are driven and independent, at least one paper, and open-source or side projects documented publicly. The one warning attached is reputational: don't join a lab, ask many questions, then disappear.

Kyars adds only that the advice "served me very well"; the replies argue about whether 85% is a real rule of thumb and whether the guidance still holds in the LLM era, which Karpathy has not answered in this thread.

## Why it matters

The 85% framing is a resource-allocation rule, not a grade target: it says the marginal hour of test prep has far lower expected value than the marginal hour of shipped work, which is the same trade-off anyone building AI systems makes between polishing a benchmark and getting something into users' hands. It also doubles as a hiring lens — the guide's emphasis on initiative, public artifacts, and not vanishing mid-project is close to what frontier-lab and agent-native hiring posts actually screen for.

## Key points

- Target 85% in every course: enough for roughly a 4.0 without over-studying, leaving time for work that compounds.
- Sleep ~7.5 hours (4 minimum) and start studying days ahead; suggested budget is ~3 days for midterms, ~6 for exams.
- Understanding != reproduction: close the book and rewrite the derivation to find out whether you actually know it.
- Study alone first, collaborate later, and teach weaker students — don't cluster only with the strongest.
- Look at past exams before studying; for math, exercises beat rereading; write a cheat sheet even if you can't use it.
- Go to office hours at least once before a final; professors sometimes say more one-on-one.
- Spend the freed time on internships, research with a professor, at least one paper, and documented open-source or side projects.
- A professor's letter calling you driven, motivated and independent "completely dwarfs" grades.
- Reputation risk: joining a research project and then disappearing after asking many questions is the biggest pet peeve on the other side.
