# Product Management Is Still About Telling Stories

**Source:** Josh Elman, 2026-09-14. A long-form essay on how AI changes the product manager's job, told through his own career at RealNetworks, LinkedIn, and Twitter.

Elman's anchor is Reid Hoffman's interview question: what is the artifact a product manager produces? His young answer was the spec — and he wrote a 120-page one for LinkedIn's jobs experience. His mature answer is that the spec is not the artifact; the story is. A spec enumerates what a system must do and which boxes must be checked, while a story explains who will use the thing, why it matters in their lives, and must be repeatable enough that it survives being passed from person to person without the PM in the room.

His claim about AI is narrower than the usual "everything changes": the cost of making things has collapsed, but the cost of judgment has not moved at all. Because a turn around the build loop used to be expensive, teams invented rituals — specs, scoping, costing, design — largely to protect scarce engineering time. With prototypes now nearly free, the loop inverts from spec-and-scope to build-and-play: build a rough version with AI to feel it, then design it properly now that you know what it is, then ship and learn. The spec stops being the deliverable, which is now literally true rather than merely aspirational.

The equal and opposite failure is treating a demo as a product. Elman insists the distance from prototype to something real still takes time, and warns that going faster tempts teams to cram everything in — "AI slop" for product. With building cheap, deciding what to build is the whole job, and the PM's question shifts from "does it fit the schedule?" to "does it fit the product?" The rest of the essay is craft: define vision as purpose, core actions, and cycle; count only direct traffic that performs core actions, not signups or App Store rank; and spend disproportionate effort on onboarding, which is where the curious "fuzzy middle" converts. His Twitter example — the 2009 "Learn Flow" that taught tweets, following, and the timeline one concept at a time — is the proof case, and he notes it moved retention more than anything else shipped that year.

## Why it matters

For anyone building AI products, the essay locates the durable constraint: generation is cheap, so differentiation moves to taste, curation, and knowing which of ten possible things to build. It also inverts the usual AI-native advice — instead of "ship fast," it says prototype fast and slow down your judgment. Practically, the onboarding argument matters most for chat-shaped products: the blank prompt box is described as the worst onboarding screen ever designed, and the fix is teaching capabilities one concept at a time to a concrete first win, measured by next-day and next-week return rather than flow completion.

## Key points

- Reid Hoffman's test: engineers produce code, BD produces contracts, designers produce the look — the PM's artifact is the story, not the spec.
- Elman wrote a 120-page spec at LinkedIn before concluding the story is the artifact and the spec is not.
- The product loop inverts: idea → spec → costing → build becomes idea → build a prototype → design → ship and learn.
- "Demos are almost free now. Working products are not" — prototype-to-product distance is unchanged.
- Vision definition: purpose (why someone picks it up), core actions (what they actually do), cycle (how often each action recurs).
- LinkedIn's core action might happen once or twice a year; the network was optimized for accurate profiles and being found, not daily engagement.
- Measure direct traffic and core-action completion, not DAU claims, signups, waitlists, ARR, or token volume.
- Onboarding targets the "fuzzy middle" between eagers and fly-bys; more simple discrete steps beat fewer complex ones (A/B tested across companies).
- Twitter's 2009 Learn Flow — welcome page, "this is a tweet," follow-to-build-timeline, then your own timeline — moved retention more than any other ship that year.
- AI products now yield literal transcripts of the user journey; read them, don't outsource opinion-forming to a summarizer.
